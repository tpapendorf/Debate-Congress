# AI Learning Aid: A Resolution to Punish Crimes Against Humanity

## Summary
Urges the United States to ratify the Rome Statute and join the International Criminal Court, which prosecutes genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity and aggression.

## Affirmative arguments
### Joining shows the U.S. means what it says
Warrant: The U.S. helped create international war crimes courts but stays outside the ICC.
Impact: Membership strengthens U.S. credibility on human rights.

### A stronger ICC deters atrocities
Warrant: More members mean more support for investigations and arrests.
Impact: Fewer leaders get away with mass crimes.

### Membership lets the U.S. shape the court
Warrant: Members vote on judges, prosecutors and budgets.
Impact: The U.S. can protect its interests from inside.

## Negative arguments
### U.S. troops could face foreign prosecution
Warrant: Americans serving abroad could be charged by a court outside the U.S. system.
Impact: That threatens sovereignty and military operations.

### Congress has already rejected it
Warrant: The American Service-Members' Protection Act limits cooperation with the ICC.
Impact: Ratification would require reversing settled law.

### The ICC has limited success
Warrant: It has few convictions and depends on states to arrest suspects.
Impact: Joining may not reduce crimes against humanity.

## Likely questions
### Would U.S. soldiers be tried by the ICC?
- For: aff
Answer: Only if the U.S. itself won't investigate; the court is a last resort.

### Why ratify now when Congress passed laws against cooperation?
- For: aff
Answer: Times have changed, and international justice needs support.

### Has the ICC actually convicted major war criminals?
- For: neg
Answer: Neg should press on the low conviction count.

### Does ratification need two-thirds of the Senate?
- For: either
Answer: Yes. Treaties require a two-thirds Senate vote.
