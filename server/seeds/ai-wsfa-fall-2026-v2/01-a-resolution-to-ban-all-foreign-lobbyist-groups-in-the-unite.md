# AI Learning Aid: A Resolution to Ban All Foreign Lobbyist Groups in The United States

## Summary
The resolution asks Congress to ban lobbying "on behalf of foreign governments, political parties, or other foreign principals." Today the Foreign Agents Registration Act (FARA, 1938) does the opposite: it lets that lobbying happen as long as the agent registers and discloses. The Congressional Research Service says plainly that FARA "neither prohibits representation of foreign interests in the United States nor prevents dissemination of foreign propaganda."

Three things in the text drive this debate. First, "other foreign principals" is a FARA term that reaches foreign companies and even individuals abroad, not just governments. Second, the people who would be banned are mostly American citizens working at American firms, which is where the First Amendment fight lives. Third, it is a resolution, so it has no definitions, penalties or enforcement agency. Expect the clash to come down to transparency versus prohibition: does disclosure work well enough, or is foreign money in policymaking a harm no amount of paperwork fixes?

## Affirmative arguments
### Disclosure tells us foreign influence is happening, but it does nothing to stop it
Warrant: FARA only requires a filing. In 2022 and 2023 alone, registered foreign agents reported nearly 130,000 political activities, such as meetings, calls and emails with officials, plus $14.3 million in campaign contributions (Quincy Institute). Every one of those was legal because it was disclosed. A filing that a voter will never read does not change whose interests get heard.
Impact: Foreign governments get sustained, professional access to lawmakers that most American constituents never get. That access shapes arms sales, sanctions and trade rules that affect Americans directly.
Proof: Quincy Institute evidence card; Washington Examiner/OpenSecrets card on Japan's $48.5 million in 2024.
They'll say: "Disclosure lets the press and voters hold people accountable."
Respond: Disclosure only works if someone is watching and the rules are enforced. In February 2025 the Attorney General disbanded the Foreign Influence Task Force and limited FARA criminal charges to conduct "similar to more traditional espionage" (CRS). The watchdog has been told to stand down.
Best speech slot: Authorship or first affirmative.

### The worst cases show the system catches people only after the damage is done
Warrant: Senator Bob Menendez was convicted in 2024 of acting as an agent of the Egyptian government while chairing the Senate Foreign Relations Committee. Michael Flynn and Paul Manafort both did undisclosed foreign work. In each case, the disclosure system failed in real time, and the public only learned after prosecutors stepped in. A bright-line ban is easier to see and enforce than a paperwork rule: if you are paid by a foreign government to lobby, you are breaking the law, full stop.
Impact: The integrity of the people writing foreign policy. When the chair of the committee that oversees aid to Egypt is working for Egypt, the harm is not hypothetical.
They'll say: "Those were crimes under existing law. The system worked."
Respond: It worked years late and only for the people who got caught. The CRS notes exempt and unregistered agents do not appear in the FARA database at all. We are measuring the iceberg by its tip.
Best speech slot: Early affirmative or a mid-round rebuttal against "FARA works."

### Congress already accepts that foreign money and self-government don't mix
Warrant: Federal law bans foreign nationals from contributing to U.S. campaigns, and the Supreme Court upheld that ban in Bluman v. FEC (2012, summarily affirmed). The court's reasoning was that the U.S. has a compelling interest in "limiting the participation of foreign citizens in activities of American democratic self-government." Lobbying for laws is as much a part of self-government as funding campaigns. This resolution extends a principle we already accept.
Impact: It answers the constitutional attack before it lands, and it gives the affirmative a principle to weigh: self-government belongs to Americans.
They'll say: "Bluman was about elections, not petitioning Congress."
Respond: Concede the case was about contributions, then ask the room why paying a firm $10 million to lobby a senator is more protected than giving that senator's campaign $100.
Best speech slot: Any affirmative speech after the first negative raises the First Amendment.

## Negative arguments
### The ban silences Americans, not foreigners, and will likely be struck down
Warrant: The people who would be banned are U.S. citizens at U.S. firms, and the First Amendment protects their right to "petition the Government." Bluman upheld limits on foreign nationals' own spending in elections. It did not approve banning American citizens from advocacy because of who their client is. Even the CRS describes U.S. foreign-influence law as having "generally favored transparency over censorship," and in Meese v. Keene (1987) the Supreme Court upheld FARA specifically because it required labels rather than suppressing speech.
Impact: If the courts strike the ban down, we lose both the ban and the political will for real reform. We end up with weaker protection than we have now.
They'll say: "Congress can regulate foreign governments more strictly."
Respond: Agreed, and FARA already does. The question is whether Congress can jail an American lawyer for representing Ukraine. Ask the affirmative to name one court that has upheld a total ban on advocacy by U.S. citizens.
Best speech slot: First negative.

### "Other foreign principals" sweeps in allies, companies and humanitarian work
Warrant: FARA defines a foreign principal to include foreign governments, foreign political parties, foreign companies and individuals outside the U.S. The text bans all of them. That means Ukraine could not hire anyone to make its case for aid, Taiwan could not lobby on arms sales, and a German automaker could not explain a trade rule to Congress. Many of the countries that lobby hardest are allies. Japan was the top FARA spender in 2024 ($48.5 million), mostly through its trade promotion agency.
Impact: U.S. policy gets made with less information from the people it affects, and our allies lose a voice while our adversaries keep using covert channels.
They'll say: "Allies can use their embassies and ambassadors."
Respond: Embassies already do, and they still hire lobbyists because a few diplomats cannot reach 535 offices. And ask: if Ukraine's ambassador can make the same argument, what is the ban actually stopping?
Best speech slot: Early negative. Strong in a room that supports Ukraine or Taiwan.

### A ban pushes influence underground, where we lose the transparency we have
Warrant: Foreign governments will not stop wanting to influence Washington. If the legal, disclosed path is closed, they shift to think tank funding, U.S. subsidiaries, consultants who claim exemptions, or covert agents. The CRS notes that exempt agents already do not appear in the FARA database. A ban grows that blind spot. Under disclosure, reporters can at least see that the UAE paid Akin Gump $881 million over 25 years (Quincy).
Impact: Disclosed influence can be debated and countered. Hidden influence cannot. The ban trades a known problem for an unknown one.
Best speech slot: Mid-round negative, especially as a response to the "Menendez" argument: Menendez was hidden influence, which a ban makes more common, not less.

## Likely questions
### Is the lawyer who would be banned an American citizen?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, to the author or any affirmative speaker.
Then ask: So the First Amendment protects that lawyer's right to petition Congress, correct?
Then ask: Can you name a court that has upheld banning a U.S. citizen from advocacy because of who the client is?
Goal: Get the concession that the ban restricts Americans' speech, then pivot to "it will be struck down" in your speech.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Yes, and Congress has always been allowed to regulate who an American may be paid to act for. Bluman upheld limits on foreign participation in self-government, and acting as a paid agent of a foreign government is conduct, not just speech."

### Under this resolution, could Ukraine hire anyone to explain why it needs U.S. aid?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Then ask: What about Taiwan on arms sales?
Then ask: So the countries hurt first are our allies?
Goal: Make the room picture who loses. Keep it yes or no.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Ukraine has an embassy and an ambassador who meets with members every week. What changes is that they can't pay a former senator $50,000 a month for access. Diplomacy stays, purchased access goes."

### What does "other foreign principals" include?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, early in the round.
Then ask: Does it include a foreign-owned company?
Then ask: A foreign charity?
Goal: The author has to either admit the ban reaches companies and charities, or admit they don't know their own text. Both help you.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "It uses FARA's definition. The core of the resolution is governments and parties, and Congress can write carve-outs for commercial trade advocacy when it drafts the bill."

### If FARA already requires disclosure, what new harm does a ban stop that disclosure misses?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, to a mid-round affirmative.
Goal: Forces the affirmative to explain why disclosure fails rather than repeating that foreign influence is bad.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Disclosure tells us after the fact. Nearly 130,000 contacts were reported in two years, and all of them were legal. The harm is the access itself, not the secrecy."

### Did the Menendez case happen under the disclosure system or in spite of it?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, to a negative who says FARA works.
Then ask: So the chair of Foreign Relations was secretly working for a foreign government, and disclosure didn't stop it?
Goal: Undercut "FARA works" with the most famous recent failure.
Best answer (if you're negative): "In spite of it, which is my point. He hid it. A ban doesn't stop people who are already willing to break the law. It only stops the ones who disclose."

### Which agency enforces this, and what is the penalty?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Goal: Expose that the resolution has no enforcement. Use it only once; then move on.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "It's a resolution, so it states the policy Congress should adopt. DOJ's FARA unit already exists and would enforce the bill that follows."
