# AI Learning Aid: A Bill to Restrict the Levying of Tariffs on Canada

## Summary
The bill bars the executive branch from putting tariffs on Canada without prior approval from Congress, defined as passing a tax under Congress's Article I, Section 8 power. It enforces this through "the impeachment of any official found in conflict" and "the striking down of any tariff imposed without Congressional approval." It takes effect 90 days after passage.

Big recent context: on February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court ruled 6 to 3 in Learning Resources v. Trump that the emergency powers law (IEEPA) "does not give the President authority to impose tariffs." That struck down the fentanyl and border tariffs on Canada, Mexico and China. But the ruling didn't touch other laws Congress has passed that do let the President set tariffs, such as Section 232 (national security, used for steel, aluminum, autos and lumber), Section 301 and Section 122. Right after the ruling, a 10% global tariff took effect under Section 122, with goods covered by the U.S.-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA, called CUSMA in Canada) exempt.

Canada was the top destination for U.S. exports in 2024, with total goods trade of about $762 billion (USTR). Two textual issues decide a lot: impeachment can't be triggered automatically by a statute, and Congress can't "strike down" a tariff by itself without passing a law. The clash: restoring Congress's constitutional tax power and protecting our closest trading partner versus tying the President's hands in negotiations and national security.

## Affirmative arguments
### The Constitution gives tariff power to Congress, and the Supreme Court just confirmed it
Warrant: Article I, Section 8 gives Congress the power to "lay and collect Taxes, Duties, Imposts and Excises." The Congressional Research Service, summarizing the Court's ruling, notes the President has no inherent peacetime authority to impose tariffs. Presidents have used broad laws passed decades ago to raise taxes on imports without a vote. This bill restores the Constitution's design for our largest export market.
Impact: Tariffs are taxes paid by American importers and consumers. Those decisions should be made by the people's elected representatives, in public, with a vote.
They'll say: "Congress delegated that power by law; the President is using it legally."
Respond: Delegation can be taken back, and that's what this bill does for one critical relationship. Congress has every right to decide how much of its own power to hand over.
Best speech slot: Authorship.

### Unpredictable tariffs on Canada hurt American businesses and workers first
Warrant: Canada was the top destination for U.S. exports in 2024, buying about $350 billion in U.S. goods, and two-way goods trade was about $762 billion (USTR). Supply chains for cars, energy and food cross the border many times. When tariffs appear and disappear by executive order, American companies can't plan, and Canada retaliates against U.S. exports.
Impact: Higher prices for American families and lost sales for American farmers and manufacturers, especially in border states like Washington.
They'll say: "Tariffs protect American industry."
Respond: Maybe some tariffs do. The bill doesn't ban them; it requires a vote. If a Canada tariff is a good idea, it should be able to pass Congress.
Best speech slot: Early affirmative.

### A predictable process strengthens the U.S. position in the USMCA review
Warrant: The USMCA is up for its joint review in 2026. Partners negotiate more seriously when they know commitments will hold. Requiring Congress to approve tariffs on Canada makes U.S. promises more credible, because they can't be undone overnight.
Impact: A stable deal with a top trading partner and closest ally, instead of a cycle of threats and retaliation.
Best speech slot: Mid-round affirmative.

## Negative arguments
### The enforcement section is unconstitutional on its face
Warrant: Section 3 enforces the bill through "the impeachment of any official found in conflict with this legislation." Impeachment is a process in the Constitution: the House votes to impeach, and the Senate needs two-thirds to convict. A statute can't make it automatic or require it. Section 3 also says Congress will "strike down" tariffs, but Congress can't cancel an executive action without passing a new law, which the President can veto. Striking down actions is what courts do.
Impact: The bill's only enforcement tools don't work, so it changes nothing in practice.
They'll say: "It just means Congress will use its existing powers."
Respond: Then the section adds nothing, and the bill relies on the same powers Congress has today and hasn't used.
Best speech slot: First negative.

### It takes away national security tools for one country only
Warrant: Section 232 lets the President act when imports threaten national security, such as steel or aluminum needed for defense. The Supreme Court left those laws in place. This bill strips those tools only for Canada. If Chinese steel is routed through Canada, or a crisis requires quick action, the President would have to wait for Congress.
Impact: Slower responses to real threats, and a loophole that other countries could exploit by shipping through Canada.
They'll say: "Congress can act quickly in an emergency."
Respond: In fall 2025, Congress went through a 43-day government shutdown before it could pass a funding bill. Trade emergencies move faster than that.
Best speech slot: Early or mid negative.

### It weakens U.S. leverage right when negotiations are happening
Warrant: The USMCA joint review is happening in 2026. Negotiators get concessions partly because the other side knows the U.S. can act. Announcing that the President can't impose tariffs on Canada without a vote of Congress hands Canada a stronger hand at the table. The Fraser Institute, a Canadian think tank, called the review "even more critical" for Canada after the ruling.
Impact: Worse terms for American workers in the most important trade deal the U.S. has.
Best speech slot: Mid or late negative. Good for crystallization.

## Likely questions
### Can a law require the House to impeach someone?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, to the author.
Then ask: And can Congress "strike down" a tariff without passing a law the President signs?
Then ask: So what actually enforces this bill?
Goal: The strongest line in the debate. The answer to the last question is "the courts," which the author didn't write in.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "No, impeachment stays a choice of the House and Senate. The bill makes clear that violating it is an impeachable offense. And any tariff without approval would be illegal, so importers could challenge it in court, exactly like they did with IEEPA."

### Does the Supreme Court's ruling stop the President from using Section 232 tariffs on Canada?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Then ask: So Congress already decided to give the President this power?
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "No, 232 still stands. That's why this bill is needed: the Court closed one door, and the President can still open others."

### Why only Canada, and not Mexico or every country?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Canada is our largest export market, our closest ally and our partner in NORAD. Starting with the most important trade relationship is a reasonable first step."

### Who pays a tariff on Canadian lumber: Canada, or the American company that buys it?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, to any negative speaker.
Goal: Get the negative to admit tariffs are paid by Americans at the border.
Best answer (if you're negative): "The importer pays it at the border, yes. But tariffs also pressure Canada to make concessions, which is the point of using them in negotiations."

### What does Article I, Section 8 say about who sets duties?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, friendly question to the author.
Best answer (if you're the author): "It gives Congress the power to 'lay and collect Taxes, Duties, Imposts and Excises.' Tariffs are duties. The Constitution puts them with Congress."

### If Chinese steel is shipped through Canada, could the President respond quickly under this bill?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Customs already enforces rules of origin, so Chinese steel doesn't become Canadian by passing through. And the USMCA review is the place to tighten those rules."
