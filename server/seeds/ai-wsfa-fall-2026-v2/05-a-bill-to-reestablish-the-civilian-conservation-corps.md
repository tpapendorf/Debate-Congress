# AI Learning Aid: A Bill to Reestablish the Civilian Conservation Corps

## Summary
The bill brings back the Civilian Conservation Corps as a voluntary program for U.S. citizens aged 18 to 25. Members get a job, training, housing, meals, basic medical care and a monthly stipend in exchange for conservation work: reforestation, wildfire prevention, erosion control, trails, watershed and habitat restoration, and park facilities. Congress appropriates $5.5 billion a year: $2.5 billion for participants, $2 billion for projects and equipment, and $1 billion for residential camps. The Department of Labor runs it with Agriculture and Interior, starting July 1, 2028.

The original CCC (1933 to 1942) enrolled about 3 million young men, peaked at about 500,000 in 1935, paid $30 a month with $25 sent home to families, and planted over 2 billion trees. It was created when about one in four workers was unemployed.

Notice what the text leaves out: how many people it enrolls, how much the stipend is, and how it relates to the roughly 150 existing conservation corps, like the Washington Conservation Corps, that already serve about 20,000 young adults a year. The clash is need versus design: is the wildfire and land-management backlog big enough to justify a new national corps, or is this a costly duplicate built for a 1933 problem?

## Affirmative arguments
### The wildfire crisis needs boots on the ground before fires start
Warrant: The federal government already spends more than $3 billion a year fighting wildfires, and Forest Service researchers project that could rise to nearly $4 billion a year by 2050, and to about $13 billion a year in the most severe scenario (Boise State Public Radio, reporting a federal climate risk report). Prevention work, like thinning, fuel breaks and clearing brush, is labor-intensive and is exactly what the bill funds. Every dollar of prevention reduces the fires we pay billions to fight.
Impact: Fewer destroyed homes, fewer smoke days and lower firefighting costs. In Washington, this is not abstract: every summer brings wildfire smoke across the state.
They'll say: "We should fund the Forest Service directly instead."
Respond: The Forest Service needs workers to do the treatment, and this bill supplies them. The two work together; the bill's $2 billion for projects goes to work on public lands those agencies manage.
Best speech slot: Authorship.

### It gives young adults paid training, a path to careers and a reason to stay connected to work
Warrant: The bill offers employment, vocational training, housing and a stipend. For an 18-year-old without college plans, that's a paycheck, a skill set (chainsaw certification, equipment operation, fire crew experience) and a résumé. The original CCC gave over 3 million people new skills (National Park Service).
Impact: Builds the next generation of wildland firefighters and land managers at a time when those agencies struggle to hire.
They'll say: "Unemployment is low; this isn't 1933."
Respond: The goal isn't emergency jobs; it's skills and service. And conservation work has to be done regardless of the unemployment rate.
Best speech slot: Early or mid affirmative.

### Conservation corps work is proven, not experimental
Warrant: Washington's own conservation corps has shown measurable results: at restoration sites, native plant cover rose 9.6% versus 0.4% at comparison sites, and noxious weeds fell 15.6% versus 1.4% (AmeriCorps research). The original CCC built much of the infrastructure of our state and national parks, including campgrounds still in use at Acadia.
Impact: Answers "will this work?" with evidence. Scaling a model that already works is lower risk than inventing a new one.
Best speech slot: Mid-round affirmative.

## Negative arguments
### The bill spends $5.5 billion a year without saying how many people it serves
Warrant: The text sets the money but not enrollment, stipend size or cost per participant. $2.5 billion a year goes to participants, plus $1 billion a year for camps. For comparison, the existing network of about 150 conservation corps serves about 20,000 young adults a year (The Conversation). If the new CCC serves a similar number, that's well over $100,000 per participant per year on participant costs alone. If it serves far more, the bill must explain where and how.
Impact: Billions of taxpayer dollars every year with no measure of success. Without enrollment targets, there's no way to hold the program accountable.
They'll say: "The Department of Labor will set the details."
Respond: Then the most important number in the program, how many people it serves, isn't in the bill Congress is voting on.
Best speech slot: First negative.

### It duplicates programs that already exist, including in Washington
Warrant: AmeriCorps, the Corps Network's roughly 150 member programs and state corps like the Washington Conservation Corps already do the same work. When the Biden administration launched the American Climate Corps in 2023, critics noted it "may largely duplicate existing conservation corps" (The Conversation). Building new federal camps from scratch costs $1 billion a year that could instead expand programs already running.
Impact: Money spent building duplicate infrastructure is money not spent on actual conservation work.
They'll say: "Existing programs are too small."
Respond: Then expand them. That's faster and cheaper than building new camps, which won't open until 2028.
Best speech slot: Early or mid negative.

### The 1933 model was built for a different problem
Warrant: The original CCC was emergency relief at 25% unemployment, sending most of each paycheck home to families. Today the labor market is very different, and residential camps that require young adults to leave home may not fit people who have jobs, school, or families. The bill limits eligibility to citizens aged 18 to 25, excluding older workers and veterans who join many current corps.
Impact: A program designed for the past may struggle to recruit today, and Congress would pay for empty bunks.
Best speech slot: Mid or late negative. Good for crystallization if the affirmative relies on nostalgia.

## Likely questions
### How many participants will this program enroll?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, to the author.
Then ask: So we're appropriating $5.5 billion a year without knowing how many people it serves?
Then ask: What's the cost per participant?
Goal: Expose the missing number. Don't let the author substitute a historical number for a real answer.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "The Department of Labor sets enrollment based on approved projects, so it scales to the work that needs doing. At $2.5 billion for participant costs, that's tens of thousands of young people a year."

### How is this different from AmeriCorps or the Washington Conservation Corps?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Scale, housing and a single national mission. Existing corps serve about 20,000 people across 150 programs with uneven funding. This bill builds a national workforce for public lands, and can partner with those corps."

### How much does the federal government spend fighting wildfires each year?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, as a friendly question to the author.
Best answer (if you're the author): "More than $3 billion a year on average, and federal researchers project it rising to $4 to 5 billion, or as high as $13 billion in a severe scenario."

### Why does the bill exclude a 26-year-old veteran who wants to serve?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "The original CCC focused on young adults because that's when job training changes a career path the most. A friendly amendment adding veterans would be welcome."

### If prevention saves money on firefighting, would you agree the program pays for part of itself?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, to a negative speaker.
Goal: Force the negative to either deny that prevention works or concede the savings.
Best answer (if you're negative): "Prevention saves money when it's done. The bill doesn't require any acres to be treated or any measure of success, so there's no guarantee of savings."

### Why does a conservation program need $1 billion a year for camps?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Because the work is in remote forests and parks far from where young people live. Housing on site is what made the original CCC work, and it makes the program open to people who can't afford rent."
