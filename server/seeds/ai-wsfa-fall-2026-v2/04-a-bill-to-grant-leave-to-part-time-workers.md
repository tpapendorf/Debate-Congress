# AI Learning Aid: A Bill to Grant Leave to Part-Time Workers

## Summary
The Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA, 1993) gives up to 12 weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave for a new child or a serious illness. To qualify today, you must work for an employer with 50 or more employees within 75 miles, for at least 12 months, and for at least 1,250 hours in the last year (about 24 hours a week). This bill replaces the 12-month and 1,250-hour requirements with "90 days of service." The Department of Labor implements it, six months after passage.

Read Section 2 carefully, because it is the best material in this debate. It amends 3 U.S.C. § 412 and 5 U.S.C. §§ 6381 and 6382. Those sections cover employees of the Executive Office of the President (Title 3) and federal civil servants (Title 5). The rule for private-sector workers, where nearly all part-time workers are, is 29 U.S.C. § 2611, and Section 2 never touches it. Section 1 says the FMLA "shall be amended" generally, so the affirmative will lean on Section 1. Also notice what the bill does not change: the 50-employee rule stays, and the leave stays unpaid.

The clash: does widening eligibility meaningfully help low-wage, part-time workers, or does it burden employers while missing the workers it claims to help?

## Affirmative arguments
### The hours rule is built to exclude part-time workers, who need leave just as much
Warrant: In 2018 only 56% of U.S. employees were eligible for FMLA. About 28% of all employees failed the tenure or hours test (Abt Associates for the Department of Labor). The 1,250-hour line is about 24 hours a week, which a part-time cashier or a parent working school hours will never reach. Illness and childbirth don't check your schedule first.
Impact: A worker can lose their job for having a baby or getting cancer. Low-wage workers are hit hardest: only 38% of workers earning under $15 an hour were eligible, compared with 63% of those earning more.
They'll say: "Part-time workers can negotiate leave with their employer."
Respond: Low-wage part-time workers have the least bargaining power of anyone in the economy. That's the reason the FMLA exists at all.
Best speech slot: Authorship.

### Unpaid job protection costs employers little and keeps workers attached to work
Warrant: The FMLA doesn't require pay. It requires that the job be there when the worker comes back. For employers already covered (50+ employees), adding more workers to the same rules means the same paperwork they already do. Keeping a trained worker is usually cheaper than hiring and training a replacement.
Impact: Fewer workers forced out of the labor force by a medical event, and less reliance on public assistance after a job loss.
They'll say: "This is a burden on employers."
Respond: The bill doesn't touch the 50-employee threshold, so small businesses are exactly as exempt as they are today.
Best speech slot: Early or mid affirmative, after the first negative raises employer costs.

### Single parents are the most likely to fall through the gap
Warrant: In the same Department of Labor survey, only 43% of workers in single-parent households were FMLA-eligible, versus 63% in dual-parent households (Abt Associates). Single parents are more likely to work part time or switch jobs, which resets the 12-month clock.
Impact: The people with no backup caregiver are the least protected. A 90-day rule protects them far sooner.
Best speech slot: Mid-round affirmative. Pairs well with a personal, concrete example in your introduction.

## Negative arguments
### As written, the bill changes leave rules for White House staff and federal employees, not private-sector part-timers
Warrant: Section 2 amends 3 U.S.C. § 412, which covers Executive Office of the President employees, and 5 U.S.C. §§ 6381 and 6382, which cover federal civil servants. The eligibility rule for private-sector workers is 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2), and the bill never amends it. Federal employees already have generous leave. The workers the bill is named for, part-time retail and service workers, are almost all in the private sector.
Impact: The bill may not help the people it promises to help, and passing a law that targets the wrong statute is how bad legislation gets made.
They'll say: "Section 1 says the FMLA shall be amended to 90 days."
Respond: Section 1 states a goal; Section 2 is the actual amendment. When a bill gives specific instructions, those control. Ask the author to name the section of the U.S. Code that covers a Target employee.
Best speech slot: First negative. Use it early and let later negative speakers extend it.

### It keeps the 50-employee rule, so most excluded workers stay excluded
Warrant: About 22% of all employees are ineligible because their worksite is too small, alone or in combination with other reasons (Abt Associates). Many part-time workers work at small businesses like restaurants and local shops. The bill doesn't change that rule, so they get nothing.
Impact: The bill claims to fix the coverage gap while leaving one of its biggest pieces untouched.
They'll say: "You can't fix everything in one bill."
Respond: Fair, but then the affirmative can't claim to have solved coverage for part-time workers. Weigh the actual benefit, not the title.
Best speech slot: Early or mid negative.

### Unpaid leave is a right many low-wage part-time workers can't afford to use
Warrant: The FMLA gives time off without pay. A worker earning $15 an hour for 20 hours a week can't go 12 weeks without income. The Bipartisan Policy Center argues that job protection should be paired with paid leave to actually reach low-wage workers. Meanwhile, employers face 12-week absences from staff with as little as 90 days on the job and few hours a week.
Impact: Real costs on employers, small practical benefits for workers. If we want to help part-time workers, paid leave is the target.
Best speech slot: Mid or late negative. Strong in crystallization because it accepts the goal and attacks the method.

## Likely questions
### Which section of the U.S. Code sets FMLA eligibility for a private-sector employee?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, to the author.
Then ask: Does your bill amend 29 U.S.C. § 2611?
Then ask: So Section 2 changes the rules for White House staff and federal employees?
Goal: The single strongest line of questioning in this debate. Stay calm and let the text speak.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Section 1 directs that the FMLA's work requirements be lowered to 90 days, which covers every FMLA employee, including 29 U.S.C. § 2611. Section 2 makes conforming changes for federal workers. The intent is clear."

### Does this bill change the 50-employee requirement?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Then ask: So a part-time worker at a 30-person restaurant gets nothing?
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Correct, and that's deliberate. It protects small businesses, which answers the negative's own burden argument."

### Is the leave paid?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Then ask: Can a worker earning $300 a week afford 12 weeks without pay?
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "No, it's unpaid. But today that worker can be fired for taking even one week. Job protection comes first; paid leave can build on it."

### Under the bill, could someone working 5 hours a week for 90 days take 12 weeks of protected leave?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, to show the bill has no hours floor.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Yes, if they have a serious medical condition or a new child. The job is protected, not paid, so the employer's cost is holding a part-time position open."

### What share of low-wage workers qualify for FMLA today?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, as a friendly question to the author.
Best answer (if you're the author): "Only 38% of workers earning under $15 an hour, according to the Department of Labor's own survey."

### If a part-time worker has a baby after six months on the job, what protects their job today?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, to a negative speaker.
Goal: Force the negative to say "nothing" out loud.
Best answer (if you're negative): "State law in many states, including Washington's Paid Family and Medical Leave program, which is paid. That's my point: states are doing this better."
