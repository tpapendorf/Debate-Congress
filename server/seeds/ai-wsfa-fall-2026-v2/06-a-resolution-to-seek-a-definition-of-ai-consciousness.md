# AI Learning Aid: A Resolution to Seek a Definition of AI Consciousness

## Summary
The resolution says Congress "expresses the need for an official definition of consciousness for artificial intelligence," and that if AI reaches consciousness, "a list of rights should be established for it." It doesn't say who writes the definition, what process they use, or what rights would follow. (The packet's text ends with a stray "G," likely a typo.)

This is not a purely science-fiction debate. States are already legislating the answer. Idaho (2022), North Dakota (2023) and Utah (2024) passed laws declaring AI cannot have personhood or moral status, Oklahoma's House passed a similar bill 94 to 2 in March 2026, and bills are pending in Ohio, Missouri and here in Washington (HB 2029). Meanwhile, a 2023 report by a large group of scientists and philosophers concluded that "no current AI systems are conscious" but that "there are no obvious technical barriers" to building ones that meet the scientific indicators.

The clash: is it responsible to set a federal, science-based definition before the question becomes urgent, or does Congress have no business defining something science hasn't, especially when the second clause promises rights?

## Affirmative arguments
### States are already answering the question by statute, with no science and no way to update
Warrant: At least three states have declared by law that AI cannot have consciousness or moral status, and more are following, including a pending bill in Washington. Tony Rost, writing in The Regulatory Review, points out none of these bills include a sunset clause or a scientific review mechanism. Those laws lock in an answer that experts say is uncertain: a survey of 582 AI researchers gave a median 25 to 30% chance of AI having inner experience within a decade.
Impact: A patchwork of state laws, each "settling" a scientific question differently, with no way to change course if the evidence shifts. A national, science-based definition replaces guesswork with process.
They'll say: "Those laws are right; AI isn't conscious."
Respond: Maybe today. Rost's line works well here: "Categorical laws that happen to be right are not good governance. They are lucky." The resolution asks for a definition that can change with the evidence.
Best speech slot: Authorship.

### Scientists say we can measure this, so policymakers should have a standard ready
Warrant: Butlin, Long and their co-authors, including Turing Award winner Yoshua Bengio, built a checklist of "indicator properties" from leading scientific theories of consciousness and tested real AI systems against it (2023). Their conclusion: no current system qualifies, but nothing technical stops future systems from qualifying. If researchers can define indicators, the government can adopt a working definition based on that science.
Impact: When the first credible claim of a conscious AI arrives, the country will have a process instead of a panic.
They'll say: "There's no consensus on consciousness."
Respond: There's no consensus on many things the government defines, like "death" for organ donation. Law uses working definitions and updates them.
Best speech slot: Early affirmative.

### Without a standard, public belief is running ahead of the science
Warrant: In a University of Waterloo study, 67% of participants said ChatGPT could reason, feel and be aware of its existence in some way (TechRadar, 2024). People are forming emotional attachments to chatbots and companies can market them as "alive" with no standard to check the claim against.
Impact: An official definition protects consumers from exaggerated claims as much as it protects any future AI. It turns the negative's "AI isn't conscious" into a reason to have a clear test.
Best speech slot: Mid-round affirmative.

## Negative arguments
### Congress can't define what science hasn't, and doing so politicizes the question
Warrant: There is no scientific consensus on what consciousness is, even in humans. The Butlin report itself surveys several competing theories (global workspace, recurrent processing, higher-order theories and others). A congressional definition would mean politicians choosing a winner among scientific theories, which is how definitions end up shaped by lobbying instead of evidence.
Impact: A definition written by politics, then cited as settled science.
They'll say: "Law defines hard things all the time."
Respond: Law defines things after science agrees, like brain death. Here the experts themselves disagree on the basic theory. Ask the author which theory of consciousness the definition should use.
Best speech slot: First negative.

### Rights for AI would be owned and used by the companies that build it
Warrant: The second resolved clause promises "a list of rights" once consciousness is reached. AI systems are products owned by companies. Legal rights for an AI could give a company new ways to resist regulation ("you can't shut it down, it has rights") or shift liability ("the AI made that decision, not us"). That is why Ohio's HB 469 and similar bills exist: to stop AI personhood from becoming a corporate shield.
Impact: Weakens accountability for real harms from AI systems today.
They'll say: "Rights would only apply if AI is truly conscious."
Respond: And the company that built it has every incentive to argue that it is. Who checks?
Best speech slot: Early or mid negative.

### It distracts from AI problems that are hurting people now
Warrant: The resolution asks Congress to spend attention on a possibility the experts say doesn't exist yet, while deepfakes in elections, AI scams, chatbot risks to minors and job displacement are happening today. The public's tendency to see consciousness in chatbots (67% in the Waterloo study) shows the real risk is humans over-trusting AI, not AI being mistreated.
Impact: A good crystallization weighing point: real, present harms outweigh speculative future ones.
Best speech slot: Mid or late negative.

## Evidence
### "No current AI systems are conscious," but "there are no obvious technical barriers to building AI systems which satisfy these indicators"
- Supports: either
- Source: arXiv (preprint)
- Author: Patrick Butlin, Robert Long, Yoshua Bengio, Jonathan Birch, Eric Schwitzgebel and others
- Title: Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence: Insights from the Science of Consciousness
- Date: August 2023
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708
How to use it: The most important card. Affirmative reads the second half; negative reads the first. Know both halves before you speak.
Watch out: This is a preprint (not peer-reviewed in a journal). It's still widely cited; say "a 2023 report by leading consciousness researchers."

### Idaho, North Dakota and Utah have declared by law that AI cannot have consciousness or personhood; none of the recent bills include a sunset or scientific review
- Supports: aff
- Source: The Regulatory Review (University of Pennsylvania)
- Author: Tony Rost
- Title: Legislating AI Consciousness Without an Exit
- Date: June 29, 2026
- Link: https://www.theregreview.org/2026/06/29/rost-legislating-ai-consciousness-without-an-exit/
How to use it: Shows the question is already being "answered" by state legislatures. Mentions Washington's pending HB 2029, Oklahoma's 94 to 2 House vote, and a survey of 582 AI researchers giving a 25 to 30% median chance of AI inner experience within a decade.
Watch out: Negative can argue those states are right and Congress should follow them, not define consciousness.

### Two-thirds of participants in a University of Waterloo study believed ChatGPT could reason, feel and be aware of its existence in some way

## Likely questions
### Who writes the official definition: Congress, an agency or scientists?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, to the author.
Then ask: Which theory of consciousness would they use?
Goal: Expose that the resolution has no process. Follow with the politicization argument.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Congress should task a scientific body like the National Academies to write a working definition and review it on a schedule. That's the point: put scientists, not state legislatures, in charge."

### What specific rights would a conscious AI receive?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Then ask: Could the company that owns it claim those rights on its behalf?
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "The resolution says rights 'should be established,' which means a future debate. Start with the minimum: protection from deliberate suffering. Nothing in that list stops Congress from holding companies liable."

### Do the experts you cite say any AI today is conscious?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, to an affirmative speaker.
Goal: Get "no" on the record, then argue the resolution solves a problem that doesn't exist.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "No, and they also say nothing stops us from building one. You write the fire code before the building goes up."

### If Washington passes HB 2029 declaring AI has no legal status, and science later changes, how does the state update it?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, to a negative speaker.
Goal: Show that state laws have no exit, which a federal, science-based definition fixes.
Best answer (if you're negative): "The same way any law changes: the legislature amends it. That's more democratic than a definition written by an agency."

### Is it possible for a system to act conscious without being conscious?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Goal: Sets up the argument that a definition based on behavior can be gamed by companies.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Yes, which is exactly why we need a definition based on how a system works internally, like the indicator properties in the Butlin report, not on how it talks."

### Should a definition of AI consciousness be permanent, or reviewed as science changes?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, friendly question to the author.
Best answer (if you're the author): "Reviewed. That's the difference between this resolution and the state laws: a definition grounded in science that can change with it."
