# AI Learning Aid: A Bill to Require the Pentagon to Pass an Audit

## Summary
Any Pentagon component that fails to get a clean audit opinion loses 10% of its budget for every year it keeps failing, starting July 1, 2027. The money goes back to the Treasury. Independent accounting firms hired by the DoD Inspector General decide pass or fail, with the GAO overseeing.

The Pentagon has failed all eight audits it has ever had (2018 through 2025) and is the only one of the 24 major federal agencies never to pass. In December 2025 it reported $4.65 trillion in assets and 26 material weaknesses. Congress already set a goal of a clean audit by December 31, 2028, in the FY2024 defense bill.

What to watch in the text: the penalty is 10% with no exemptions, while every serious congressional proposal so far has used 0.5% to 1% and protected troop pay and health care. "For every year" may compound. And it starts in mid-2027, before the 2028 deadline the Pentagon is already working toward. The clash will be accountability versus readiness: is a big stick the only thing that will finally work, or does it punish troops for accountants' failures?

## Affirmative arguments
### Eight straight failures prove goals without consequences don't work
Warrant: The Pentagon has been audited every year since 2018 and has failed every time. The FY2024 NDAA set a 2028 deadline, but Taxpayers for Common Sense notes that goal "included no accountability mechanisms or consequences." Leaders keep restating the same target. Agencies change when their budgets are on the line, because budget is the one thing every component head is measured by.
Impact: The Department manages $4.65 trillion in assets and still cannot fully say what it owns. Every year without a clean audit is a year Congress votes on the largest discretionary budget in government without reliable numbers.
They'll say: "The Pentagon is already making progress and targets 2028."
Respond: Federal agencies have been required to produce auditable statements since the CFO Act of 1990, and the 2010 defense bill set a goal of audit readiness by 2017. Deadlines without consequences have been the story for over 30 years. The bill doesn't stop progress; it rewards it, because any component that passes keeps all its money.
Best speech slot: Authorship.

### The penalty only hits components that fail, so it creates a race to pass
Warrant: The bill applies component by component. Nine of 28 components already earned clean opinions in 2024. Those agencies lose nothing. The rest now have a clear reason to fix their books first, because no component chief wants to explain a 10% cut. The penalty is targeted at the exact offices that are failing, not the whole Department.
Impact: Fast, measurable progress. It also gives Congress an honest test: if a component truly cannot track its money, it should not be trusted with more of it.
They'll say: "Cuts will hurt troops."
Respond: The cut is chosen by the component's own leadership, who can cut waste, overhead and the programs that can't be audited. And the fastest way to avoid it entirely is to pass.
Best speech slot: Early affirmative, especially in response to the first negative.

### Not knowing what you own is itself a readiness problem
Warrant: The December 2025 audit flagged the F-35 program's Global Spares Pool because the Pentagon could not verify the existence, completeness or value of the parts (Defense News). If you don't know where spare parts are, you buy duplicates, or a jet sits on the ground waiting for a part you already own. Clean books are not paperwork; they are logistics.
Impact: Turns the negative's best argument. Readiness depends on knowing what you have, and this bill forces that.
Best speech slot: Mid-round affirmative, as direct refutation of "this hurts readiness."

## Negative arguments
### 10% with no exemptions is ten times what any serious proposal has used, and it can hit troop pay and health care
Warrant: The bipartisan Audit the Pentagon Act (Sanders and Grassley) uses a 1% penalty, and the 2026 House version uses 0.5% then 1%, and both exempt military personnel accounts and the Defense Health Program (Taxpayers for Common Sense; MeriTalk). This bill has no exemptions at all. The Army has failed every audit and has a budget of roughly $185 billion, so a 10% cut is in the range of $18 billion in a single year, and "for every year" means it repeats.
Impact: A penalty that large lands on paychecks, training, maintenance and military families, people who have nothing to do with bookkeeping. It punishes the soldier for the accountant's failure.
They'll say: "Leadership will choose what to cut."
Respond: The bill doesn't say that, and it doesn't protect personnel. Ask the author to point to the line that protects troop pay. There isn't one.
Best speech slot: First negative.

### Cutting the budget of a failing component takes away the money it needs to pass
Warrant: The Pentagon fails audits largely because of outdated financial and business systems. Fixing them costs money: Senator Ernst's RECEIPTS Act would spend $150 million on automation and $150 million to replace old business systems (MeriTalk). A 10% cut takes resources away from exactly the components that most need to modernize, which makes it harder to pass next year, which triggers another cut.
Impact: A death spiral. The bill could lock the worst-performing components into permanent failure while degrading their missions.
They'll say: "They'll find the money by cutting waste."
Respond: If the Pentagon knew where its waste was, it would pass the audit. That's the whole problem.
Best speech slot: Early or mid negative.

### The bill moves the deadline up and fights the plan already underway
Warrant: Current law already requires a clean audit by December 31, 2028, and the Department says it is working to that date. This bill starts penalties July 1, 2027, so the FY2027 audit, a year before the deadline, could trigger cuts. Congress would be changing the rules mid-plan. Better options exist: the RECEIPTS Act pairs penalties with rewards, like $10 billion in budget flexibility for passing.
Impact: Weighs well in crystallization: the negative can support audit accountability while showing this is the wrong tool.
Best speech slot: Mid or late negative. Good for a crystallization speech.

## Likely questions
### Does the bill exempt military pay or health care from the 10% cut?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, to the author.
Then ask: So a soldier's pay could be cut because their branch failed an accounting test?
Then ask: The Sanders-Grassley bill exempts those accounts. Why doesn't yours?
Goal: The text has no exemption. Get the author to admit it, then hammer "punishes troops" in your speech.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "The bill doesn't dictate where cuts fall, and no Congress would let troop pay go unpaid. I'd welcome a friendly amendment exempting personnel, which makes the bill stronger, not weaker."

### Does "10% for every year" mean 10% of the original budget, or 10% more each year?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Goal: Shows the author didn't define the penalty. If it compounds, three years of failure is a cut of about 27%.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "It's 10% for each year of failure measured against that year's budget. The point is the cut continues until the books are fixed."

### If a component loses 10% of its budget, where does the money come from to fix its accounting systems?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, to set up the "death spiral" argument.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "From the waste the audit would uncover. And components get to keep 100% of their budget the moment they pass, which is the strongest incentive to invest in fixing it."

### How many years has the Pentagon promised a clean audit and missed?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, to any negative speaker.
Then ask: So eight failures with no penalty. Why would a ninth year be different?
Goal: Make the negative defend the status quo. Keep it short.
Best answer (if you're negative): "It's failed eight times, and it's also gone from zero to nine components passing. The answer is to finish that work, not to cut the budget of the people doing it."

### Current law already sets a 2028 deadline. Why does your bill start penalties in 2027?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Because that deadline has no real teeth, and 2028 has been the target in every press release. Starting the clock in 2027 shows we mean it."

### Who decides what counts as a "clean audit opinion"?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, to the author, as a friendly question to show the bill is fair.
Best answer (if you're the author): "Independent public accounting firms, hired by the Inspector General under the current process and overseen by GAO. It's the same standard every other agency already meets."
