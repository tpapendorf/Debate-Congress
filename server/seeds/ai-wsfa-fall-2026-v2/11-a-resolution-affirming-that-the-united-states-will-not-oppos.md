# AI Learning Aid: A Resolution Affirming that the United States Will Not Oppose Taiwanese Self-Determination

## Summary
The resolution states that the U.S. "will not oppose any decision on Taiwan's future that the people of Taiwan reach peacefully and through democratic means," and calls on the President to reject any request from China that the U.S. declare it opposes Taiwan independence.

Some vocabulary matters here. Official U.S. policy says the U.S. "does not support" Taiwan independence and opposes unilateral changes to the status quo by either side. Beijing wants the U.S. to go further and say it "opposes" independence. On September 24, 2026, during his Washington visit, Xi Jinping urged President Trump to do exactly that (Xinhua, via Al Jazeera). Earlier in 2026, President Trump postponed final approval of a $14 billion arms package for Taiwan and called arms sales a "very good negotiating chip."

In Taiwan, an August 2026 government-commissioned poll found 83.9% say Taiwan's future should be decided by its 23 million people, and 83.6% favor maintaining the status quo. The key phrase in the text is "any decision," which includes a formal declaration of independence. China's 2005 Anti-Secession Law authorizes "non-peaceful means" if Taiwan moves toward formal independence. The clash: does this resolution defend a democracy from being bargained over, or does it change a careful policy that has helped keep the peace for decades?

## Affirmative arguments
### Saying "oppose" would hand Beijing a concession and tell Taiwan its future is up for trade
Warrant: The U.S. has never said it opposes Taiwan independence; it says it doesn't support it. Changing one word would be a major gift to Beijing at a summit, with nothing in return. It would also signal to 23 million people that Washington and Beijing can decide their future over their heads. With a $14 billion arms package already delayed as a "negotiating chip," the risk that Taiwan gets bargained away is real.
Impact: Rewards pressure and coercion. If pressure works on Taiwan policy, Beijing learns it works everywhere.
They'll say: "The resolution isn't needed; nobody has agreed to change the wording."
Respond: Xi asked for it in person in September. The time for Congress to speak is before the President answers, not after.
Best speech slot: Authorship.

### The resolution restates long-standing U.S. principles; it doesn't create new ones
Warrant: The Taiwan Relations Act of 1979 treats any effort to decide Taiwan's future "by other than peaceful means" as a threat to peace. In 2000, President Clinton said cross-Strait differences must be resolved peacefully and "with the assent of the people of Taiwan." The resolution says the same thing: peaceful, democratic, decided by Taiwan's people. It supports a process, not an outcome.
Impact: Answers the negative's "this is provocative" argument. Restating existing U.S. policy can't be a provocation.
They'll say: "'Any decision' includes formal independence."
Respond: It also includes unification, if Taiwan's people ever chose it peacefully. The resolution is neutral on the outcome. It just insists the people decide.
Best speech slot: Early affirmative, right after the first negative.

### Taiwan's people want the status quo, so standing with them is not a push for independence
Warrant: In the August 2026 Mainland Affairs Council poll, 83.6% favored maintaining the status quo and 81.2% rejected "one country, two systems" (Focus Taiwan). Taiwan's voters are not rushing toward a declaration. The resolution doesn't encourage one; it says the U.S. will respect what Taiwan's people decide.
Impact: Turns the negative's war risk argument. The pressure for change is coming from Beijing, which U.S. intelligence officials judge as the main source of tension, not from Taipei.
Best speech slot: Mid-round affirmative.

## Negative arguments
### "Any decision" includes a formal declaration of independence, which China has said it will answer with force
Warrant: The resolution says the U.S. will not oppose "any decision" reached democratically. A referendum declaring formal independence would qualify. China's Anti-Secession Law authorizes "non-peaceful means" in that situation. By promising not to oppose it, the U.S. would move from "does not support" toward implied acceptance, the exact change Beijing calls a red line.
Impact: A war over Taiwan would be catastrophic, involving two nuclear powers and the world's most advanced chip supply.
They'll say: "Taiwan's people want the status quo, so independence isn't coming."
Respond: Then why change U.S. language at all? If no one is declaring independence, the resolution only adds risk.
Best speech slot: First negative.

### Strategic ambiguity has kept the peace, and this resolution chips away at it
Warrant: Historian Peter Mansoor argues ambiguity has kept the peace across the Strait: the U.S. neither guarantees Taiwan's defense nor abandons it, which keeps Beijing guessing and discourages unilateral moves by either side (Hoover Institution, 2021). A bipartisan task force of 14 leading China experts recommended the U.S. restate that it "does not support Taiwan independence" and avoid "symbolic political gestures that needlessly aggravate Beijing" (UC San Diego, 2022).
Impact: Expert consensus favors careful, consistent language. The resolution is a symbolic gesture of the kind experts warn against.
They'll say: "Ambiguity hasn't stopped China from building up its military."
Respond: Peace has held for over 75 years. Ask the affirmative which risk is greater: China's buildup, or a U.S. statement that gives Beijing a reason to act sooner.
Best speech slot: Early or mid negative.

### Congress shouldn't make promises the public may not keep
Warrant: A May 2026 Economist/YouGov poll found fewer than 40% of Americans say the U.S. should be prepared to use military force to stop China from controlling Taiwan (Al Jazeera). The resolution signals strong support without any commitment to back it up. Diplomacy is also mainly the President's job; Congress speaking alone can send mixed messages.
Impact: Encourages Taiwan to count on a U.S. response that may not come, which is the most dangerous combination.
Best speech slot: Mid or late negative.

## Likely questions
### If Taiwan holds a referendum and declares formal independence, does this resolution commit the U.S. not to oppose it?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, to the author.
Then ask: And China's Anti-Secession Law authorizes force in that case?
Then ask: So the resolution removes a U.S. check on the one move most likely to start a war?
Goal: The strongest negative line. Stay calm and factual.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "The U.S. would not oppose a peaceful, democratic choice, which has been the spirit of U.S. policy since 1979. And Taiwan's people overwhelmingly support the status quo, so the scenario you describe isn't what they want."

### What is the difference between "does not support" and "opposes" Taiwan independence?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, friendly question to the author.
Best answer (if you're the author): "'Does not support' means the U.S. takes no side. 'Opposes' means the U.S. would actively work against what Taiwan's people might choose. Beijing wants the second one, and this resolution says no."

### Has the U.S. agreed to Xi's request?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Then ask: So this resolution responds to a change that hasn't happened?
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Not yet, and that's the point. With arms sales already treated as a negotiating chip, Congress should speak before the next summit, not after."

### Which do you trust more on this issue: 14 bipartisan China experts, or this resolution?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, after reading the UCSD task force line in your speech.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "The same experts told the U.S. to keep its language consistent. Switching from 'does not support' to 'opposes' is the inconsistent move, and that's what this resolution prevents."
Watch out: The task force wrote that the U.S. would accept any outcome "peacefully agreed by mainland China and Taiwan," meaning both sides agree. Don't claim they back Taiwan deciding alone.

### Who is the main source of rising tension in the Taiwan Strait?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, to a negative speaker.
Goal: The whereas clause says U.S. officials point to pressure from Beijing. Make the negative say it.
Best answer (if you're negative): "Beijing's pressure is the main source, which is exactly why the U.S. should avoid giving it a new excuse."

### Would you support this resolution if it only said the U.S. rejects China's request?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, to a negative who focused on "any decision."
Goal: Separates the two resolved clauses. If the negative says yes, they've agreed with half the resolution.
Best answer (if you're negative): "That's a different resolution. This one includes 'any decision,' and that's the language that creates the risk."
