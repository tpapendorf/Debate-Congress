# AI Learning Aid: A Bill to Ban Deepfake Political Advertisements

## Summary
The bill bans deepfake political ads meant to influence voting in federal elections. A deepfake is AI-made or manipulated images, video or audio "with the intent of spreading false or misleading information about another candidate, while knowing the content was artificially made." A political ad is "any type of messaging distributed by mass media, social media, or other forms of communication" meant to sway the public toward a candidate. The Federal Election Commission enforces it: a $25 million fine per violation, and a second offense bans the violator from spending on political ads for the rest of the election cycle. It starts January 1, 2027.

The threat is real. Before the 2024 New Hampshire primary, AI robocalls imitating President Biden reached thousands of voters; the consultant behind them got a $6 million FCC fine but was acquitted of all state criminal charges. Thirty states now regulate election deepfakes (Public Citizen, May 2026), mostly through disclosure labels.

But courts have struck down the strictest laws. In Kohls v. Bonta (2025), a federal judge permanently blocked California's ban as a violation of the First Amendment, and in September 2026 a judge blocked Montana's law for the plaintiffs who challenged it. And the agency in charge, the FEC, lost its quorum in April 2025 and couldn't vote on enforcement for most of the following year. The clash: protecting voters from AI lies versus free speech, satire, and whether this text can work.

## Affirmative arguments
### AI makes election lies cheap, fast and convincing, and the existing law didn't stop them
Warrant: The New Hampshire Biden robocalls show how easy this is: one consultant, a cheap AI voice tool, thousands of calls days before a primary. A New Hampshire jury acquitted him of all 22 state charges under existing voter suppression and impersonation laws (NHPR). Current law wasn't written for synthetic media. A specific federal ban closes that gap.
Impact: Voters can be misled about when or how to vote, or about what a candidate said, days before an election when there's no time to correct it.
They'll say: "The FCC fined him $6 million, so the system worked."
Respond: The FCC fine was under robocall rules. The same deepfake as a TikTok video or a mailer isn't covered by those rules at all. This bill covers all media.
Best speech slot: Authorship.

### A $25 million fine actually deters campaigns
Warrant: Campaigns spend millions on ads. A small fine is a cost of doing business. A $25 million penalty and a ban on ad spending for the rest of the cycle mean a campaign that uses a deepfake could lose the election because of it.
Impact: Deterrence before the damage is done, which matters because a lie that goes viral the weekend before Election Day can't be undone afterward.
They'll say: "The fine is too high."
Respond: It's only too high if you plan to make a deepfake. The bill requires knowing the content is fake and intending to mislead.
Best speech slot: Early affirmative.

### The intent requirement protects satire and honest speech
Warrant: The bill only covers content made "with the intent of spreading false or misleading information" by someone "knowing the content was artificially made." Obvious parody isn't meant to mislead. That's narrower than California's law, which reached satire unless it carried a large-print disclaimer.
Impact: Answers the First Amendment attack. The bill targets fraud, and fraud has never been protected speech.
Best speech slot: Mid-round affirmative, right after the negative cites Kohls v. Bonta.

## Negative arguments
### Courts keep striking these laws down, and this one goes further than California's
Warrant: In Kohls v. Bonta (2025), Judge John Mendez permanently blocked California's deepfake law, finding it content- and viewpoint-based and "not narrowly tailored," and wrote that "restricting speech should be the government's tool of last resort." He favored "counter speech, rigorous fact-checking, and the uninhibited flow of democratic discourse." In 2026 a federal judge also blocked Montana's law for the plaintiffs who challenged it, calling it "presumptively unconstitutional." California's law used disclaimers; this bill is an outright ban with a $25 million fine.
Impact: A law that will likely be struck down protects no one, and wastes the time we could spend on disclosure laws that survive.
They'll say: "This bill has an intent requirement, so it's narrower."
Respond: California's law required knowing and malicious intent too, and it still fell. Intent doesn't fix a ban on political speech based on its content.
Best speech slot: First negative.

### The definitions sweep in ordinary people and miss real threats
Warrant: "Political advertisement" means "any type of messaging" on social media meant to sway the public. That's every political post. A teenager who shares an edited meme of a candidate could face a $25 million fine. Meanwhile, "deepfake" only covers false information "about another candidate," so a candidate making a flattering deepfake of themselves, or a foreign group faking a voting-location announcement, may not be covered.
Impact: Overbroad where it hurts free speech, and underinclusive where the dangers are biggest.
They'll say: "Prosecutors won't go after teenagers."
Respond: The bill gives them the power to. A law that depends on officials choosing not to enforce it is a bad law.
Best speech slot: Early or mid negative.

### The FEC can't enforce it
Warrant: The FEC needs four commissioners to approve enforcement. It lost its quorum on April 30, 2025, and as of March 2026 it had only two members (Congressional Research Service). The FEC also deadlocks along party lines on many politically charged cases. Handing it a $25 million penalty for political speech invites partisan fights over every case.
Impact: Even if it survives the courts, the bill won't be enforced when it matters, while the threat of selective enforcement chills speech.
Best speech slot: Mid or late negative.

## Likely questions
### Could a high school student who posts an edited AI meme of a candidate be fined $25 million?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, to the author.
Then ask: Your definition of political ad is "any type of messaging" on social media, correct?
Goal: Make the overbreadth concrete and personal for a room full of students.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Only if they knew it was fake and meant to spread false information about a candidate to change votes. A joke meme everyone knows is fake isn't meant to mislead anyone."

### Does this bill cover a candidate who makes a deepfake of themselves?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "The bill targets deceiving voters about another candidate, which is the most common harm. A friendly amendment can extend it."

### California's law was struck down. What makes yours different?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Then ask: California used disclaimers; you use a ban. Isn't yours harder to defend?
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "California's law reached satire and parody and forced disclaimers on them. Ours only covers knowing deception meant to mislead voters, which is closer to fraud than to opinion."

### How many FEC commissioners are needed to enforce this law, and how many are there?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Four are needed. Nominees were sent to the Senate in 2026. A law shouldn't be judged by one year's vacancies; Congress can and should fill them."

### Should it be legal to fake a candidate's voice telling voters to stay home on Election Day?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, to any negative speaker.
Goal: An easy "no" that gets the negative to agree there's a harm.
Best answer (if you're negative): "No, and it's already illegal as voter suppression in most states and as an FCC robocall violation. We don't need a ban on all political deepfakes to stop that."

### What happened to the consultant behind the New Hampshire robocalls?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, friendly question to the author.
Best answer (if you're the author): "A $6 million FCC fine, but a jury acquitted him of all 22 state criminal charges. Existing law didn't treat it as a crime."
