# AI Learning Aid: A Bill to Restore Union Rights

## Summary
The bill makes two big changes to the Taft-Hartley Act of 1947. First, it amends Section 8(b)(4) to let unions use secondary boycotts, defined as "a strike against an employer for the purpose of forcing that employer to cease doing business with a separate employer." Second, it repeals Section 14(b), the clause that lets states pass "right-to-work" laws, and voids all existing ones. The Department of Labor enforces it, fifty days after passage.

Context: Union membership was 10.0% of U.S. workers in 2025, including just 5.9% in the private sector (BLS). Twenty-six states have right-to-work laws after Michigan repealed its law in 2024. In those states, a union contract cannot require workers to pay dues or fees, even though federal law requires the union to represent every worker it bargains for.

Two textual notes. "Fifty days" is a very short timeline to void laws in 26 states. And the bill can't reach public-sector unions on dues, because the Supreme Court's 2018 Janus decision made mandatory fees for government workers unconstitutional. The clash is worker power versus worker choice: are these the tools workers need to bargain against huge employers, or do they force people to pay a union they didn't choose and hurt neutral businesses?

## Affirmative arguments
### Right-to-work laws let workers get union benefits without paying, which starves unions of resources
Warrant: Federal law requires a union to bargain for and represent every worker in its unit, members or not. That includes paying lawyers to handle a nonmember's grievance. Right-to-work laws let workers receive all of that for free. When some workers stop paying, the union has fewer resources to bargain, and wages fall. The Economic Policy Institute finds workers in right-to-work states are paid 3.2% less than similar workers in other states, about $1,670 a year.
Impact: Lower wages and weaker bargaining for every worker, members and nonmembers alike.
They'll say: "Right-to-work states have a lower cost of living."
Respond: EPI's analysis compares similar workers and accounts for state cost of living, and the gap remains. Heritage disputes this, so be ready to explain why EPI's method is stronger.
Best speech slot: Authorship.

### Secondary boycotts are how workers reach the company that really holds the power
Warrant: Today many workers are employed through contractors, franchises and subcontractors. A delivery driver may work for a small contractor while a giant company sets their routes and pay. Under the current ban, the drivers' union can't pressure the company that actually controls the contract. Lifting the ban lets workers apply pressure where the power is.
Impact: Workers in fissured industries, like delivery, janitorial work and fast food, finally get leverage.
They'll say: "Secondary boycotts hurt businesses that aren't part of the dispute."
Respond: The "neutral" business often isn't neutral. It's the one choosing the contractor that pays poverty wages. And the bill's definition is narrow: a strike aimed at stopping business with a separate employer.
Best speech slot: Early or mid affirmative.

### Union density is at historic lows, and the 1947 rules are a big part of why
Warrant: Only 10.0% of workers were union members in 2025, and just 5.9% in the private sector (BLS). Taft-Hartley was passed over President Truman's veto and has shaped labor law for almost 80 years. Michigan's repeal of right-to-work in 2024 shows states can and do reverse course when workers organize.
Impact: Restores balance in a labor market where most workers have no one bargaining for them.
Best speech slot: Mid-round affirmative.

## Negative arguments
### Workers shouldn't be forced to pay an organization they didn't choose to join
Warrant: Repealing Section 14(b) means a union contract can require every worker to pay dues or fees as a condition of keeping their job. That's a worker who voted against the union, or was hired years after the vote, being made to fund it. The Supreme Court in Janus (2018) called this a First Amendment problem for government workers; the same principle of choice applies to private workers.
Impact: Workers lose the right to decide whether a union earns their money. Unions that must earn support are more accountable to members.
They'll say: "They get the benefits, so they should pay."
Respond: Unions choose to be exclusive bargaining representatives; that's the trade they make. The answer to free-riding is members-only bargaining, not forced dues.
Best speech slot: First negative.

### It overrides 26 states' laws in 50 days
Warrant: Twenty-six states chose right-to-work through their legislatures or voters. The bill voids all of them fifty days after passage. Businesses chose locations in those states based on those laws. Economist Thomas Holmes found a large, abrupt jump in manufacturing activity when crossing from non-right-to-work to right-to-work states (Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis, 1996).
Impact: Disrupts state economies and investment decisions, and replaces state choices with one national rule.
They'll say: "Labor law is already federal."
Respond: And Congress chose in 1947 to leave this one decision to states. The affirmative has to justify taking it away.
Best speech slot: Early or mid negative.

### Secondary boycotts spread one dispute across the economy
Warrant: Congress banned secondary boycotts in 1947 because they let a union shut down businesses with no connection to the labor dispute. A strike against a supplier, distributor or shipping company to force it to stop dealing with the targeted employer can ripple through supply chains. Consumers and workers at neutral businesses pay the price.
Impact: Product shortages, lost wages for workers at neutral firms and economic disruption far beyond the original dispute.
Best speech slot: Mid or late negative.

## Likely questions
### Under your bill, can a worker who voted against the union be fired for not paying it?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, to the author.
Then ask: Even if they were hired after the vote and never had a say?
Goal: Frame the bill as forced payment. Keep it yes or no.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "They can be required to pay a fair share for the representation the law requires the union to give them. That's not unique: you pay for public schools whether or not you voted for the school board."

### Does federal law require unions to represent workers who don't pay dues?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, to a negative speaker.
Then ask: So right-to-work states require unions to work for free?
Best answer (if you're negative): "Unions choose exclusive representation. If they don't want to represent nonmembers, they can push for members-only bargaining."

### Can a secondary boycott shut down a business that has no dispute with its workers?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Only if that business keeps doing business with an employer that refuses to treat workers fairly. That's a choice, and it's the same kind of pressure consumers use when they boycott."

### Why fifty days?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Then ask: Is fifty days enough time for 26 states to unwind their laws?
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Once Section 14(b) is repealed, state right-to-work laws simply stop applying. There's nothing to unwind; contracts can be renegotiated as they expire."

### If a union delivers higher wages, why do workers need to be required to pay for it?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, to a mid-round affirmative.
Goal: Make the affirmative explain why voluntary support isn't enough. It's a hard question to answer cleanly in 30 seconds.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "For the same reason we don't let people skip taxes because they like roads: when the benefit goes to everyone, some people will let others pay. The law makes the union represent everyone, so everyone pays their share."

### Does this bill change dues rules for teachers and other government workers?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, to show the bill's limits.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "No. Janus covers public workers. This bill is about private-sector workers, where 94% have no union today."
