# AI Learning Aid: The Get the FLOCK Out Act

## Summary
The bill withholds 10% of a state's federal highway formula funds (23 U.S.C. § 104(b)) every year unless the state bans automated license plate readers (ALPRs) for every use except three: toll enforcement, finding stolen vehicles, and finding missing or endangered people. It takes effect in fiscal year 2028, with the Department of the Treasury overseeing it. "Flock" refers to Flock Safety, whose cameras are used by more than 5,000 law enforcement agencies.

Three things to know before you speak. First, this is a spending-power bill: Congress can't ban ALPRs in states directly, so it attaches a condition to highway money, the same tool it used for the 21 drinking age. In South Dakota v. Dole (1987) the Supreme Court upheld withholding 5% of highway funds as "relatively mild encouragement." This bill uses 10%. Second, the allowed uses do not include felony warrants or violent crime investigations. Third, Washington passed its own ALPR law in 2026 (SB 6002) with a 21-day data limit and a ban on immigration enforcement use, but it still allows felony uses, so Washington would likely lose 10% of its highway funds under this bill.

The clash: privacy and abuse of a mass-surveillance network versus a tool police say solves real crimes, plus whether Washington should force every state into one rule.

## Affirmative arguments
### Plate readers build a searchable record of where everyone drives, and it's already being misused
Warrant: ALPRs photograph every passing car, not just suspects, and networks like Flock let agencies search each other's data. That makes abuse easy. A University of Washington Center for Human Rights report found 8 Washington agencies shared data directly with U.S. Border Patrol and 10 more were accessed through "back doors" without formal authorization. One Yakima County user searched 89 networks and listed "ICE" as the reason (Axios Seattle). Elsewhere, a Texas sheriff's office searched for the car of a woman who had an abortion (NPR, citing 404 Media).
Impact: A record of where people live, worship, protest and get medical care, available to agencies the community never agreed to share with.
They'll say: "That's misuse; the answer is better rules, not a ban."
Respond: Washington already had rules against that sharing, and the UW report found agencies may have broken them anyway. When the rules fail, limiting the tool is the only reliable fix.
Best speech slot: Authorship.

### The bill keeps the uses that matter most
Warrant: The three allowed uses, stolen vehicles, missing or endangered people (including Amber Alerts) and tolls, are the cases where ALPRs are most clearly effective. The National Institute of Justice review found ALPRs increase arrests and stolen vehicle recoveries, but their broader impact on crime prevention is "unclear" because rigorous evaluations are limited (Koper and Lum, 2018).
Impact: Keeps the proven benefits and removes the dragnet uses the evidence doesn't support.
They'll say: "Police use them to solve shootings and kidnappings."
Respond: Kidnappings are covered: the bill allows searches for missing and endangered people. For other crimes, police still have warrants, witnesses and traditional investigation.
Best speech slot: Early affirmative. Best right after the first negative claims "this stops police from finding criminals."

### Communities are already rejecting these networks
Warrant: NPR reports at least 30 localities deactivated cameras or canceled Flock contracts since early 2025, including Cambridge, Massachusetts, Eugene, Oregon, and Flagstaff, Arizona. A crowdsourced map has located more than 76,000 plate readers nationwide. In Washington, Renton suspended its plate reader cameras in April 2026 (Kent Reporter).
Impact: The public is ahead of the law. A national floor protects people in states that haven't acted.
Best speech slot: Mid-round affirmative.

## Negative arguments
### Withholding 10% of highway money is coercion, and it punishes drivers
Warrant: The Supreme Court upheld a 5% highway penalty in Dole as "relatively mild encouragement," and in NFIB v. Sebelius (2012) struck down a condition that threatened about 10% of a state's overall budget as coercive (Cornell LII). This bill doubles Dole's penalty, every year. The money it withholds pays for roads, bridges and safety projects.
Impact: States either give up a policing decision or let their roads decay. Drivers pay either way.
They'll say: "Dole upheld exactly this tool."
Respond: Dole upheld 5%, and the Court said the condition must relate to the purpose of the funds. Drinking age relates to highway safety. How does a police camera policy on a city street relate to highway construction money?
Best speech slot: First negative.

### The bill's exceptions leave out the most serious crimes
Warrant: The bill allows only tolls, stolen vehicles and missing or endangered persons. It does not allow searching for a car tied to a homicide, a shooting, a felony warrant or a hit-and-run. Plate readers were credited with locating the Brown University shooting suspect, who was found dead at a New Hampshire storage facility in December (NPR). Even Washington's new privacy law, SB 6002, allows ALPR use for felony warrants and felony investigations, because legislators decided those uses are worth keeping.
Impact: Murders, shootings and hit-and-runs become harder to solve. And Washington, which just passed one of the strictest ALPR laws in the country, could still lose 10% of its highway funds.
They'll say: "States can push to add exceptions."
Respond: Not under this bill. The exceptions are written into federal law, and the penalty applies if a state allows anything else.
Best speech slot: Early or mid negative. Use the Washington SB 6002 point; it is specific and local.

### States are already regulating, and Washington's law shows the better path
Warrant: Washington's SB 6002 (2026) limits ALPR data retention to 21 days, bans use for immigration enforcement, bans cameras near schools, places of worship, health care facilities and food banks, prohibits selling data, and gives people a right to sue (Senate Bill Report). That is targeted regulation: it addresses the abuses the affirmative describes while keeping the tool for serious crimes.
Impact: A stronger crystallization frame: regulate the data, don't ban the tool. Each state can learn from the others.
Best speech slot: Mid or late negative.

- Supports: neg

Watch out: The affirmative can argue 10% of highway money is still far less than 10% of a state's whole budget, so it may survive under NFIB.

## Likely questions
### Under this bill, can police use a plate reader to find the car of a murder suspect?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, to the author.
Then ask: What about a hit-and-run driver who killed a child?
Then ask: So those cases get harder to solve?
Goal: The most effective question in this debate. Make the exceptions concrete.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Not by searching a mass database of every driver, no. Police can still get warrants for phone records, use witnesses and video, and if the suspect has taken a hostage or a child, that's a missing or endangered person, which is covered."

### Does Washington's new ALPR law satisfy this bill?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Then ask: So Washington, one of the strictest states, still loses 10% of its highway funds?
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Washington would need to narrow its felony exception. Given that the UW report found agencies sharing with Border Patrol despite existing rules, a narrower law is exactly what's needed."

### How much highway money did the Supreme Court approve withholding in South Dakota v. Dole?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Then ask: Your bill doubles that, every year?
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "5%, yes. But in NFIB the line for coercion was about 10% of a state's entire budget. Ten percent of highway formula funds is a small fraction of that."

### Why does the Department of the Treasury oversee a highway funding bill?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, quick drafting point.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Treasury handles the withholding of federal funds. The Department of Transportation would still administer the highway programs. It's a technical detail an amendment can clean up."

### Should a local police officer be able to search hundreds of other agencies' plate data and type "ICE" as the reason?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, to any negative speaker.
Goal: Get the negative to condemn the abuse, then argue only this bill stops it everywhere.
Best answer (if you're negative): "No, and Washington's new law makes that illegal, with a right to sue. That's the right fix: regulate the misuse without banning the tool for murders and shootings."

### How long does Flock keep plate data, and who can search it?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, friendly question to the author.
Best answer (if you're the author): Be careful: Flock's default practices vary by contract and state law. Answer with what you can prove: Washington now caps it at 21 days, and the UW report found federal agencies searched Washington data.
