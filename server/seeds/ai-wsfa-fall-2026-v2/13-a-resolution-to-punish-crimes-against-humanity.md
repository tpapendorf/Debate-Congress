# AI Learning Aid: A Resolution to Punish Crimes Against Humanity

## Summary
The resolution urges the future ratification of the Rome Statute so the United States becomes a member of the International Criminal Court (ICC). The ICC, based in The Hague, tries individuals for genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes and the crime of aggression. About 125 countries are members.

The history matters. President Clinton signed the Rome Statute on December 31, 2000, but said he would not send it to the Senate until U.S. concerns were addressed. In 2002, Congress passed the American Service-Members' Protection Act, which restricts U.S. cooperation with the Court and states that the U.S. "will not be bound by any of its terms." In February 2025 the U.S. began sanctioning ICC officials; by mid-2026 the chief prosecutor and eight judges were under U.S. sanctions (Carnegie Endowment).

Two textual points. First, a treaty is ratified by the President with the consent of two-thirds of the Senate, 67 votes, so this resolution can only urge. Second, the title says "punish crimes against humanity," but the resolution's only action is ICC membership; the debate is about the Court, not about whether atrocities are bad. The clash: does joining strengthen justice and U.S. leadership, or does it expose Americans to a foreign court without U.S. constitutional protections?

## Affirmative arguments
### The ICC only acts when national courts won't, so it protects Americans who are tried at home
Warrant: The Rome Statute is built on "complementarity" (Article 17): the ICC can only take a case when a country is unwilling or genuinely unable to investigate or prosecute it. The U.S. has a functioning military justice system and federal courts. If the U.S. investigates its own personnel in good faith, the ICC has no case.
Impact: Answers the negative's main fear. Membership doesn't hand Americans to The Hague; it reinforces the U.S. duty to hold its own people accountable, which the U.S. already claims to do.
They'll say: "The ICC decides whether our investigation was good enough."
Respond: That's true for every member, including the UK, France and Germany, whose troops have served alongside ours. None has had a soldier taken to The Hague over a case it investigated at home.
Best speech slot: Authorship.

### The U.S. already relies on the ICC when it's convenient, and that inconsistency costs credibility
Warrant: Carnegie's Stewart Patrick notes the U.S. has quietly supported the Court's pursuit of adversaries like Vladimir Putin and Bashar al-Assad, while sanctioning its prosecutor and judges when cases touch the U.S. or its allies (Carnegie, July 2026). The European Union called attacks on the Court "simply not acceptable." A country that supports justice only for its rivals can't credibly lead on human rights.
Impact: U.S. moral authority is a strategic asset. When we look selective, adversaries use it as propaganda and allies lose trust.
They'll say: "Supporting specific cases doesn't require joining."
Respond: It does if we want a vote. Members elect the judges and prosecutor and set the budget. Today the U.S. has influence only by threatening the Court from outside.
Best speech slot: Early or mid affirmative.

### A permanent court deters atrocities that would otherwise go unpunished
Warrant: Before the ICC, international justice depended on one-off tribunals like Nuremberg, Yugoslavia and Rwanda, created after the atrocities. A standing court means leaders know accountability is possible before they act. The resolution's whereas clause notes that the U.S. "has a commitment to punishing these international crimes."
Impact: Joining adds U.S. investigative capacity, funding and legitimacy to the institution, making that deterrent stronger.
Best speech slot: Mid-round affirmative.

## Negative arguments
### Americans tried at the ICC would lose constitutional protections, including a jury trial
Warrant: The ICC doesn't use juries; panels of judges decide guilt. Congress found in the American Service-Members' Protection Act that Americans prosecuted by the ICC would lack Bill of Rights protections like trial by jury (22 U.S.C. § 7421). Ratification would place U.S. service members and officials under a court the Constitution doesn't govern.
Impact: A soldier sent abroad by the U.S. government could be tried without the rights every American is promised at home.
They'll say: "Complementarity means the ICC defers to U.S. courts."
Respond: The ICC decides when it defers. It already opened an investigation into alleged crimes by U.S. personnel in Afghanistan, while the U.S. wasn't even a member. Joining only strengthens its claim.
Best speech slot: First negative.

### The crime of aggression could put U.S. leaders' security decisions on trial
Warrant: Congress's findings warn that senior officials could face prosecution for national security decisions, especially under a definition of aggression adopted over U.S. objections, and that such a definition could undercut the UN Security Council's role (22 U.S.C. § 7421). A President ordering a strike to stop a terrorist plot could be accused by a court the U.S. doesn't control.
Impact: Weaker U.S. ability to act quickly in a crisis, and a court with political incentives deciding when force was legal.
They'll say: "No court has charged a U.S. leader with aggression."
Respond: Not yet. Treaties last for decades, and the U.S. can't predict who the prosecutor will be or what cases will be politically popular.
Best speech slot: Early or mid negative.

### Ratification is politically impossible and would collide with existing U.S. law
Warrant: Ratification needs 67 senators. The current administration has sanctioned the ICC's prosecutor and eight of its judges, Secretary Rubio said the Court "threatens every aspect of our political and legal system," and the U.S. has signed more than 100 bilateral agreements not to surrender each other's nationals to the Court (Carnegie). The resolution doesn't say how to reconcile any of this.
Impact: Weighs well in crystallization: the resolution urges a step that cannot happen and that conflicts with federal law, while doing nothing for victims now.
Best speech slot: Mid or late negative.

## Likely questions
### Would an American soldier tried at the ICC have the right to a jury?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, to the author.
Then ask: So ratifying this treaty means an American could be tried without a right the Constitution guarantees?
Goal: Make the constitutional cost concrete.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "No, the ICC uses judges. But the ICC only takes a case if the U.S. refuses to investigate it. American soldiers would be tried in American courts, as long as we do our job."

### Under complementarity, who decides if a U.S. investigation was genuine?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Then ask: So the ICC, not the U.S., gets the final word?
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "The ICC judges decide, the same way they decide for Britain and France. That's what makes it a court instead of a political body, and the U.S. would help elect those judges."

### Has the ICC already tried to investigate Americans while the U.S. was not a member?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Goal: Show the Court asserts jurisdiction over Americans regardless, then argue joining strengthens that claim.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Yes, over alleged crimes in Afghanistan, which is a member. That's the point: the Court can already act. Joining gives the U.S. a seat at the table to shape how it does."

### Should the U.S. support the ICC's case against Vladimir Putin?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, to any negative speaker.
Then ask: Then why should the U.S. reject the Court's authority when it applies to us?
Goal: Expose inconsistency. Expect a confident "yes" to the first question.
Best answer (if you're negative): "Supporting one case is different from giving up sovereignty. The U.S. can share evidence against Putin without putting our own citizens under the Court's power."

### How many senators must vote to ratify a treaty?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Then ask: Given the current sanctions on the Court, how many votes does this treaty have today?
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Sixty-seven. That's why it says 'future ratification.' Resolutions like this build the case over time; it took the U.S. 40 years to ratify the Genocide Convention."

### What does the resolution do to punish crimes against humanity today?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "It commits Congress to the institution that does the punishing. Without a permanent court, atrocity prosecutions depend on tribunals created after the fact."
