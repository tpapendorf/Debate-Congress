# AI Learning Aid: A Bill to Regulate Data Centers

## Summary
The bill sets national rules for building and operating data centers. Every data center must be within 2 miles of a populated area, can't use drinking water for cooling, can't exceed 200,000 square feet, must prove it "will not disrupt any wildlife," must employ over 200 people for over 12 months, and must be "voted on and justified by the state." Violations carry fines up to $400 million. The Department of Energy, FERC and the EPA enforce it, one year after passage.

Read Section 2 closely: a data center is defined as a facility of at least "100,00" square feet (likely meant to be 100,000). Put that together with the 200,000 square foot cap, and the bill only allows data centers between 100,000 and 200,000 square feet, while anything smaller isn't covered at all. Large "hyperscale" campuses can exceed 2.5 million square feet, so in practice the bill bans them.

The stakes are real. Data centers used 4.4% of U.S. electricity in 2023 and could use 6.7% to 12% by 2028 (Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory). A hyperscale center can consume about 550,000 gallons of water a day. Washington is a data center state, especially in Grant County. The clash: these facilities have real costs that communities can't see, but are these specific rules a fix, or a ban dressed up as regulation?

## Affirmative arguments
### Data centers are growing faster than the grid and water systems can handle
Warrant: Data center electricity use tripled from 58 terawatt-hours in 2014 to 176 TWh in 2023, and the Department of Energy's lab projects 325 to 580 TWh by 2028, driven by AI (LBNL). Utilities have to build power plants and transmission lines to serve that load, and those costs can be spread across every customer's bill. Without national rules, communities compete for projects and accept terms they can't afford.
Impact: Higher electric bills, strained grids during heat waves, and fossil fuel plants kept running longer to meet demand.
They'll say: "Data centers bring investment and tax revenue."
Respond: Much of that revenue is given back. Good Jobs First found states paid about $1.95 million in subsidies per permanent data center job. That's not investment; that's a giveaway.
Best speech slot: Authorship.

### Banning drinking water for cooling protects communities in drought
Warrant: A mid-sized data center uses about 300,000 gallons of water a day and a hyperscale center about 550,000, mostly lost to evaporation (UC Berkeley Center for Law, Energy & the Environment, 2026). Equinix, a large operator, got 94% of its water from municipal sources in 2024. Alternatives exist: recycled wastewater, closed-loop liquid cooling and air cooling. The bill pushes the industry toward them.
Impact: Drinking water stays for people. About one-fifth of U.S. data centers drew water from stressed watersheds in 2021.
They'll say: "Air cooling uses more electricity."
Respond: Recycled water and closed-loop systems don't. The bill bans potable water, not cooling.
Best speech slot: Early affirmative.

### Communities deserve a say before a facility locks in decades of resource use
Warrant: The bill requires the state to vote on and justify each data center. Today, projects are often negotiated privately between companies and local officials under nondisclosure agreements, and residents learn about them after the deal is done. A public state decision makes the costs visible.
Impact: Democratic accountability for decisions that affect power bills and water for 20 years or more.
Best speech slot: Mid-round affirmative.

## Negative arguments
### The size cap and jobs rule work together as a ban on modern data centers
Warrant: The bill caps data centers at 200,000 square feet, while hyperscale campuses can exceed 2.5 million square feet (Revelio Labs). It also requires 200+ employees for 12 months. Data centers are highly automated and employ few permanent workers. Good Jobs First's $1.95 million subsidy-per-job figure exists precisely because they create so few jobs. The affirmative cannot use the "few jobs" argument and also claim a 200-employee rule is workable.
Impact: New U.S. data center construction largely stops. Projects move overseas or to many small sites, losing efficiency and raising energy use per computation.
They'll say: "Companies can build several smaller centers."
Respond: Then the bill multiplies sites, land use and construction instead of reducing them. Ask the author how many 200,000 square foot buildings it takes to replace one hyperscale campus.
Best speech slot: First negative. Push the contradiction between the size cap and the jobs rule.

### The definition creates a loophole and the standards can't be met
Warrant: The bill only covers facilities of at least 100,000 square feet. Anything smaller, say 99,000, faces none of these rules, so companies can build clusters of small facilities to avoid them. Meanwhile, the standards that do apply are impossible to prove: no project can show it "will not disrupt any wildlife," and "within at least 2 miles of a populated area" doesn't say whether that's a minimum or maximum distance.
Impact: A law that's either easy to dodge or impossible to comply with, with fines up to $400 million for guessing wrong.
They'll say: "Agencies will clarify the rules."
Respond: The bill names three agencies, DOE, FERC and EPA, and doesn't say which one decides. Three agencies interpreting vague words is a recipe for years of lawsuits.
Best speech slot: Early or mid negative.

### Data center siting is a state and local power, and states are already acting
Warrant: Zoning, water rights and local land use belong to states and cities. The bill's own text admits this by requiring states to vote, then overrides them with federal size and jobs rules. Washington's data center tax program was identified by Good Jobs First as one of the most expensive in the country, which is a policy Washington can change on its own.
Impact: Weighs well in crystallization: real problems, wrong level of government, wrong tools.
Best speech slot: Mid or late negative.

## Likely questions
### How many permanent employees does a typical data center have?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, to the author.
Then ask: So how many existing data centers meet your 200-employee requirement?
Then ask: Is this a regulation or a ban?
Goal: Show the jobs rule is unworkable. If the author says "few," they've conceded it's a ban.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Many have fewer, and that's the point. If a facility gets a public subsidy and uses a town's water and power, it should provide real jobs in return. Companies that can't meet that bar shouldn't build there."

### Does "within at least 2 miles of a populated area" mean closer than 2 miles or farther than 2 miles?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Goal: Expose the drafting problem. Keep it quick and move on.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "The intent is that data centers be close enough to the communities they serve to be accountable to them. Agencies can clarify the wording in rules."

### Under Section 2, is a 99,000 square foot data center covered by this bill?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Then ask: So a company can avoid every rule by building several smaller buildings?
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Smaller facilities have smaller impacts, so a threshold makes sense. And state votes still apply to the projects that matter most."

### How much of the U.S. electricity supply could data centers use by 2028?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, as a friendly question to the author.
Best answer (if you're the author): "Up to 12%, according to the Department of Energy's own lab, up from 4.4% in 2023."

### Do you agree data centers shouldn't cool themselves with drinking water during a drought?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, to a negative speaker.
Goal: Get the negative to agree with part of the bill, then argue the rest is just about details.
Best answer (if you're negative): "Yes, and states can require recycled water, as some already do. I'm opposing the 200,000 square foot cap and the jobs rule, which turn this into a ban."

### Which of the three agencies decides whether a data center disrupts wildlife?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "The EPA, since it's an environmental standard. DOE and FERC handle energy questions. Each agency enforces the part that matches its job."
