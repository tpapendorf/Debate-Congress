# AI Learning Aid: A Bill to Lower the Federal Corporate Tax Rate to 15% for Domestic Manufacturers

## Summary
The bill cuts the federal corporate income tax rate from 21% to 15% "for all C corporations that manufacture within the United States," starting in fiscal year 2028. Manufacturing means converting raw materials into finished products at U.S. plants owned and operated by C corporations. Treasury enforces it.

Read Section 1 closely: the lower rate applies to the corporation, not to its manufacturing income. As written, a company that runs one U.S. factory could pay 15% on all its taxable income, including from software, retail or finance. Also, only C corporations qualify. Many small and family-owned manufacturers are S corporations or other "pass-through" businesses, which pay through their owners' individual taxes and get nothing from this bill.

History matters here. From 2005 to 2017, Section 199 gave manufacturers a 9% deduction on production income. It cost about $14 billion a year, and critics called it a "manufacturing tax break gone wild" because it covered coffee roasting, film production and even gift basket assembly (Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget). The 2017 tax law repealed it when it cut the rate from 35% to 21%. And in 2025, Congress created 100% immediate expensing for new factory buildings that begin construction before 2029. The clash: does a lower rate bring factories and jobs home, or is it an expensive, loophole-prone giveaway on top of incentives that already exist?

## Affirmative arguments
### Lower taxes on manufacturing profits mean more factories built here
Warrant: Companies decide where to build by comparing after-tax returns. A study of the 2017 tax cut by Chodorow-Reich, Smith, Zidar and Zwick found that a 1% increase in the after-tax value of a dollar of taxable income was associated with about 3.8% more investment, and the 2017 law raised domestic investment by about 20% for a typical affected firm (NBER, 2024). A 15% rate makes the U.S. one of the most competitive places in the developed world to manufacture.
Impact: More plants, more equipment and more manufacturing jobs in the U.S. instead of overseas.
They'll say: "The 2017 tax cut didn't raise wages much."
Respond: The same study found wages rise over time as capital builds: its model projects a 7.2% larger capital stock and higher wages after 15 years. Investment comes first; wages follow.
Best speech slot: Authorship.

### Domestic manufacturing is a national security issue
Warrant: The pandemic showed what happens when medicine, chips and protective equipment are made overseas: shortages at the worst possible time. Defense production depends on a domestic industrial base. Targeting the tax cut to companies that actually produce goods here rewards the activity the country needs most.
Impact: Supply chains that hold up in a crisis, and less dependence on China for critical goods.
They'll say: "A tax cut doesn't guarantee production of critical goods."
Respond: No single policy does. But making U.S. production more profitable shifts thousands of decisions toward building here.
Best speech slot: Early affirmative.

### It targets the cut instead of giving it to every corporation
Warrant: Instead of cutting the rate for all businesses, which the Tax Foundation estimated would cost about $2 trillion over ten years for a general 15% rate, the bill limits the cut to companies that manufacture in the U.S. That focuses the cost on the jobs and investment the country wants.
Impact: Turns the negative's cost argument: this is a smaller, targeted version of a reform that would otherwise cost far more.
Best speech slot: Mid-round affirmative.

## Negative arguments
### As written, one factory buys a 15% rate on all of a company's income
Warrant: Section 1 lowers the rate "for all C corporations that manufacture within the United States." It doesn't limit the rate to manufacturing income. A large company with a small U.S. plant could qualify for 15% on profits from everything else it does. Section 199 showed how this goes: companies relabeled activities to qualify, from coffee roasting to film production (CRFB).
Impact: Huge revenue losses that don't buy any extra manufacturing. Companies just open or buy one qualifying plant.
They'll say: "Treasury can write rules to prevent abuse."
Respond: Treasury enforces what Congress writes. The bill says the rate applies to the corporation. Ask the author whether a tech company with one U.S. assembly plant pays 15% on its software profits.
Best speech slot: First negative.

### It costs billions and the 2017 evidence shows the cost isn't paid back
Warrant: The NBER study of the 2017 tax cut found corporate tax revenue fell by about 40% over the decade, and the extra growth "does not substantially offset" the loss. Section 199, a smaller 9% deduction, cost about $14 billion a year and nearly $200 billion over ten years (CRFB). A 6-point rate cut is a much bigger break.
Impact: Larger deficits, which mean higher interest costs and pressure to cut other priorities.
They'll say: "Growth will make up the difference."
Respond: The best study of the last corporate tax cut says it didn't. Ask the affirmative for evidence that this one would be different.
Best speech slot: Early or mid negative.

### Factories already get major tax breaks, and small manufacturers are left out
Warrant: Since 2025, manufacturers can deduct 100% of the cost of new factory buildings right away if construction begins before 2029 (Section 168(n), described by Crowe). That targets new investment directly, which is cheaper and better aimed than a rate cut on profits from old factories. Meanwhile, the bill excludes S corporations and other pass-throughs, which include many small and family-owned manufacturers.
Impact: The bill mostly rewards large corporations for factories they already have, while small manufacturers get nothing.
Best speech slot: Mid or late negative. Strong in crystallization.

## Evidence
### The 2017 tax cut raised domestic investment about 20% for a typical affected firm, but corporate revenue fell about 40% and growth did not substantially offset it
- Supports: either
- Source: National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER Digest)
- Author: Gabriel Chodorow-Reich, Matthew Smith, Owen Zidar and Eric Zwick
- Title: Investment Effects of the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act
- Date: June 1, 2024
- Link: https://www.nber.org/digest/202406/investment-effects-2017-tax-cuts-and-jobs-act
How to use it: The most important card for both sides. Affirmative uses the investment numbers; negative uses the revenue loss. The model's wage increase after 15 years is only 0.9%.

### Section 199, a 9% deduction for production income, cost about $14 billion a year and covered coffee roasting, film production and gift basket assembly
- Supports: neg
- Source: Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget
- Title: The Tax Break-Down: Section 199, the Domestic Production Activities Deduction
- Date: September 4, 2013
- Link: https://crfb.org/blogs/tax-break-down-section-199-domestic-production-activities-deduction
How to use it: Shows past manufacturing tax breaks were costly and gamed. About 70% of the benefit went to C corporations.

### Since 2025, manufacturers can immediately deduct 100% of new factory building costs if construction begins before 2029
- Supports: neg
- Source: Crowe LLP
- Author: Edward Meyette
- Title: New OBBB qualified production property deduction
- Date: July 31, 2025
- Link: https://www.crowe.com/insights/tax-news-highlights/new-obbb-qualified-production-property-deduction
How to use it: Shows Congress already created a targeted manufacturing incentive. The property must be placed in service before 2031.

### A general 15% corporate rate would cost about $2 trillion over ten years
- Supports: either
- Source: Tax Foundation
- Author: Alan Cole
- Title: Trump's corporate rate of 15 percent
- Date: April 25, 2017
- Link: https://taxfoundation.org/blog/trump-corporate-rate-15-percent/
How to use it: Affirmative: this bill's targeted version costs far less. Negative: even a fraction of $2 trillion is a large cost.
Watch out: This estimate is from 2017, when the rate was 35%, so it's not a direct estimate of this bill.

## Likely questions
### Under Section 1, does a company with one U.S. factory pay 15% on all of its income?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, to the author.
Then ask: So a tech company with one assembly plant pays 15% on its software profits?
Then ask: How is that different from the Section 199 loophole?
Goal: The strongest line in the debate. Read the exact words of Section 1 before you ask.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "The intent is manufacturing income. Treasury would apply it to income from qualifying production, and a friendly amendment can state that directly."

### Why are S corporations excluded?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Then ask: So a family-owned machine shop gets nothing, but a multinational does?
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "S corporations don't pay the corporate rate at all; their owners pay individual rates, and they already got a separate deduction in 2017. This bill fixes the corporate side."

### Did the 2017 corporate tax cut pay for itself?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Not fully, but it did raise investment by about 20% for affected firms. The question is whether that investment is worth the cost, and for manufacturing, it is."

### How much did the 2017 tax cut increase business investment?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, friendly question to the author.
Best answer (if you're the author): "About 20% for a typical affected firm, according to a 2024 NBER study led by Harvard economist Gabriel Chodorow-Reich."

### Since Congress already allows 100% expensing for new factories, what does this bill add?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Expensing helps with the cost of building; a lower rate helps with the profit from running the factory for the next 30 years. Companies look at both when deciding where to build."

### Should companies that make products in America pay less tax than ones that ship jobs overseas?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, to any negative speaker.
Goal: An easy, popular framing. Make the negative argue against "made in America."
Best answer (if you're negative): "They already do, through expensing for new factories. This bill gives the same break to a company with one plant on all of its profits, which isn't the same thing."
