# AI Learning Aid: A Resolution to Streamline School Resource Officer Jurisdiction for Educational Success

## Summary
The resolution says Congress "supports prohibiting" school resource officers (SROs) from getting involved in routine, non-violent code of conduct violations, and that schools should use educators and counselors for that discipline so SROs can focus on serious threats. The whereas clauses give examples: dress code, non-violent insubordination, profanity and "minor property damage."

It is a resolution, so it doesn't change any law, set a definition of "routine," or fund the counselors it asks schools to rely on. Policing in schools is set by states, districts and local police agreements (often called MOUs), not by Congress.

The best research on this topic cuts both ways, which is why the debate is good. A 2023 national study found SROs reduce fights and threats without weapons by about 30%, but also raise arrests and police referrals by about 52%, with much bigger effects on Black students and students with disabilities. Affirmative wants to keep the safety benefit and remove the discipline harm. Negative will argue the line between "minor" and "serious" isn't clear in a real hallway, and that schools don't have the staff to take over.

## Affirmative arguments
### Officers in minor discipline turn school rule-breaking into arrest records
Warrant: When a police officer handles a dress code or profanity issue, the available tools are police tools: citations, referrals and arrests. A national study of schools that narrowly won or missed federal SRO grants found adding SROs raised arrests and law enforcement referrals by about 52%, out-of-school suspensions by 62% and expulsions by 90% (Sorensen et al., via Brookings). An arrest for "disorderly conduct" follows a student to court, and possibly to college and job applications.
Impact: Students are pushed out of school for behavior that a counselor or vice principal could handle in a conversation. Every suspension day is lost learning time.
They'll say: "Students should face consequences."
Respond: They still will. The resolution keeps discipline in place and moves it to educators. The question is whether a teenager who swore at a teacher should meet a counselor or a criminal record.
Best speech slot: Authorship.

### The harms fall hardest on Black students and students with disabilities
Warrant: In the same study, SRO-related increases were 2.5 times larger in arrests for Black students than white students, 3.3 times larger in expulsions, and nearly three times larger for students with disabilities across suspensions, expulsions and arrests (Brookings). Minor offenses like "defiance" are the most subjective categories, which is exactly where bias has the most room to work.
Impact: Removing police from the most subjective, lowest-level discipline narrows a documented equity gap without removing officers from schools.
They'll say: "That's a training problem, not a jurisdiction problem."
Respond: Training doesn't change what an officer's job is. If the officer is in the room for a dress code call, police tools are on the table. Taking them out of the room is the only way to guarantee they aren't used.
Best speech slot: Early or mid affirmative.

### Focusing SROs on real threats is what makes them effective
Warrant: The same research found SROs reduce non-weapon violence by about 30%. That is their value. Every hour an officer spends on a hat or a cell phone is an hour not spent building relationships, watching entrances or responding to threats. The resolution doesn't remove SROs; it tells them where to point their time.
Impact: Lets the affirmative claim both safety and equity. It also turns the negative's "SROs keep kids safe" argument: yes, and this lets them do more of it.
Best speech slot: Mid-round affirmative, after the negative argues for school safety.

## Negative arguments
### "Routine" and "non-violent" are not clear lines in a real school
Warrant: The resolution's own list includes "minor property damage" and "non-violent insubordination." Vandalism can be a crime. Insubordination can turn into a physical confrontation in seconds. If an SRO has to decide in the moment whether something is "routine," they will either hesitate when it matters or be accused of violating the policy. The resolution gives no definition, no threshold and no process.
Impact: Confusion at the exact moment clarity matters, and an officer who steps back from a situation that is about to escalate.
They'll say: "Schools and police can write clear MOUs."
Respond: Then that's a local decision, and they already have that power today. The resolution adds nothing a district can't already do.
Best speech slot: First negative.

### Schools don't have the people to take over this work
Warrant: The resolution relies on "qualified educational personnel" to replace SROs in discipline. The national student-to-counselor ratio was 372 to 1 in 2024 to 2025, far above the 250 to 1 the American School Counselor Association recommends. The resolution provides no money to hire anyone. Shifting work onto already overloaded counselors means less time for mental health and college support.
Impact: The students the affirmative wants to protect lose the counseling they need, and discipline still goes unhandled.
They'll say: "Counselors are better at this than police."
Respond: Agreed, which is why they should be funded first. Ask the affirmative how many counselors this resolution hires. The answer is zero.
Best speech slot: Early or mid negative.

### Congress is the wrong level to decide what a school officer does
Warrant: SROs are hired by local police departments and school districts and governed by state law and local agreements. A congressional resolution can't bind any of them, and a one-size rule ignores the differences between a rural district with one officer and a large urban district with its own police force. The research authors themselves point to local MOUs as the tool for accountability (Education Week).
Impact: Weighs well late in the round: even if the affirmative is right about the problem, the resolution is the wrong solution.
Best speech slot: Crystallization.

## Evidence
### SROs cut non-weapon violent incidents by about 30%, but raised arrests and referrals by about 52%, suspensions by 62% and expulsions by 90%
- Supports: either
- Source: Brookings Institution
- Author: Lucy Sorensen and Montserrat Avila-Acosta
- Title: Navigating the tradeoffs of police in schools
- Date: September 7, 2023
- Link: https://www.brookings.edu/articles/navigating-the-tradeoffs-of-police-in-schools/
How to use it: The single most important card in this debate. Affirmative uses the discipline numbers; negative uses the 30% violence reduction. Also reports effects 2.5 times larger in arrests for Black students and nearly three times larger for students with disabilities.
Watch out: The same study found gun-related incidents rose after SROs were added, which researchers think may reflect more reporting. Don't overclaim in either direction.

### SROs did not reduce shootings or gun offenses; only 25% of educators said more police would make them feel safer, versus over 50% for more counselors
- Supports: aff
- Source: Education Week
- Author: Caitlynn Peetz Stephens
- Title: School Police Prevent Some Violence, But Not Shootings, Research Finds
- Date: July 7, 2023
- Link: https://www.edweek.org/leadership/school-police-prevent-some-violence-but-not-shootings-research-finds/2023/07
How to use it: Shows educators themselves want counselors, not more policing. Researcher Lucy Sorensen recommends school-police agreements (MOUs) for accountability, which the negative can use for "this is a local job."

### The national student-to-school-counselor ratio was 372 to 1 in 2024 to 2025; the recommended ratio is 250 to 1
- Supports: neg
- Source: American School Counselor Association
- Title: Student-to-School-Counselor Ratio 2024-2025
- Link: https://www.schoolcounselor.org/getmedia/62807f33-a020-4c4f-ac6f-bf284803fd97/pr_ratios-24-25.pdf
How to use it: Shows schools lack the staff the resolution relies on.
Watch out: Affirmative can use the same number to argue for funding counselors, which is the resolution's goal.

### Research from the University at Albany found police in schools results in fewer fights and harsher discipline

## Likely questions
### Under this resolution, is "minor property damage" routine or serious?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, to the author.
Then ask: What about a student who breaks a window?
Then ask: Who decides, in the moment?
Goal: Show the line is unworkable. Don't argue; just let the author struggle to draw it.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "If no one is in danger, it's routine, and an administrator handles it. If the damage is part of a threat or a fight, it's a safety issue and the SRO steps in. Schools draw this line every day; the resolution just says who goes first."

### How many new counselors does this resolution fund?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Goal: The answer is zero. Use it to set up "unfunded mandate."
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "None, because it's a resolution. But it doesn't add work; it moves work from officers to educators, who already handle most discipline. And it sets the policy direction for funding bills to follow."

### Does the research show SROs make schools safer from fights and threats?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative, to an affirmative speaker.
Then ask: So removing SROs from any part of discipline risks some of that safety?
Goal: Get the 30% concession on the record.
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "Yes, about 30% fewer non-weapon violent incidents. And the resolution keeps that, because fights and threats are not routine, non-violent violations. SROs stay on exactly those."

### If an officer's presence leads to 52% more arrests, is that because there's more crime or because there's an officer?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, to a negative who defends SROs.
Goal: Make the negative explain why arrests jump when the officer arrives. The research design compares similar schools, so the officer is the difference.
Best answer (if you're negative): "Part of it is that crimes that were already happening finally get reported. Reporting a crime isn't a harm."

### Can school districts already set these limits in their agreements with police?
- For: either
Ask as: Negative.
Then ask: So what does this resolution do that a district can't do today?
Best answer (if you're affirmative): "They can, and many haven't. Congress sets national priorities through resolutions all the time, and this one tells districts and the Department of Education what the evidence supports."

### Should a student's dress code violation ever end in a police report?
- For: either
Ask as: Affirmative, short and simple.
Goal: An easy "no" that frames the whole resolution as common sense.
Best answer (if you're negative): "No, and it almost never does. That's why we don't need a congressional resolution to say so."
