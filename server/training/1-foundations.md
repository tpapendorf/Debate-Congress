# Training lesson: How a Congress session runs
- Track: foundations
- Skill: Follow the flow of a session so you know when to speak, question and vote.
- Drill: bills
- Bill section: none

## Lesson
Congressional Debate copies a legislature. Each chamber has a **docket**: the bills and resolutions it will debate, in an order the chamber agrees on. A student **presiding officer (PO)** runs the floor, and judges score each speech.

Debate on one bill goes like this:

- **Authorship or sponsorship.** The first affirmative speech introduces the bill. If you wrote it, it's an authorship; if not, a sponsorship.
- **Questioning.** After each speech, other legislators question the speaker. The first speech usually gets a longer period than later ones. Check your tournament's rules for the exact times.
- **Alternating sides.** Speeches then go negative, affirmative, negative and so on, each up to three minutes.
- **Previous question and vote.** When the chamber is ready, someone moves the previous question, which ends debate, and the chamber votes on the bill.

The PO picks speakers by **precedence**: people who have spoken least go first, and among them, whoever spoke longest ago. That means you can't speak on every bill. Choose the bills where you have something to add.

## Strong example
Before the session, Maya reads all five bills and preps two of them deeply: an affirmative on the AGOA extension and a negative on the corporate tax cut. On the other three she preps two questions each. When the tax bill comes up, she stands for the first negative because she knows the clash best there.

## Weak example
Jordan preps one speech, on the first bill only. The chamber reorders the docket, the bill comes up third, and by then four people have already made Jordan's main point. Jordan gives a speech that repeats them and asks no questions all session.

## Try it
Open one of your team's current bills. Write one sentence on what it does, one sentence on the strongest reason to pass it, and one on the strongest reason not to. Then pick which side you would want to speak on and why.

# Training lesson: What judges reward
- Track: foundations
- Skill: Know what earns high scores and rankings, so your prep goes where it counts.
- Drill: prep
- Bill section: none

## Lesson
Judges score each speech and rank the best legislators in the chamber at the end. Ballots differ by tournament, but they look for the same things:

- **Argument quality.** A clear claim, a reason it's true, and why it matters.
- **Evidence.** Facts from named, credible sources, cited out loud.
- **Clash.** After the first couple of speeches, the best speakers answer what others said instead of starting over.
- **Delivery.** Speaking to the room, not reading. Confident, clear and paced.
- **Questioning.** Sharp, short questions, and direct answers when you're questioned.
- **Being a good legislator.** Moving debate forward, following the rules, being courteous.

A strong three-minute speech usually has a short introduction with a hook, two points (sometimes one well-developed point, or three short ones), and a quick conclusion. Two well-supported points beat four thin ones.

Rankings reward the whole session, not one speech. A legislator who gives two strong speeches and asks good questions on every bill often ranks above someone with one great speech who disappears the rest of the time.

## Strong example
"Representatives, the question on this bill isn't whether we want factories at home. Everyone here does. It's whether a six-point rate cut is the way to get them. My first point answers Representative Lee directly..." The speaker frames the debate, then clashes by name.

## Weak example
"Today I'm going to talk about four reasons we should pass this bill. First, the economy. Second, jobs. Third, China. Fourth, it's fair." Four labels and no reasons, and nothing connects to what anyone else said.

## Try it
Look at a speech plan you already have, or start one. Cut it down to your two strongest points. For each, write the one fact you would cite and the speaker you expect to answer.
