# Training lesson: Speak from notes, not a script
- Track: delivery
- Skill: Deliver a three-minute speech from a short outline while keeping eye contact.
- Drill: practice
- Bill section: none

## Lesson
Congress speeches are **extemporaneous**: prepared, but not read word for word. Reading a script is one of the easiest ways to lose points, because you can't look at the room and you can't adapt to what was just said.

Try this setup:

- **One sheet or card**, with an outline, not sentences. Your hook, two points (each with its fact and source), and your closing line.
- **Write out only the first sentence and the last sentence.** Those are the ones nerves make you forget.
- **Look up at the end of every sentence.** Glance down for the next point, then look at a judge or a legislator.

Delivery checklist:

- **Pace.** Slow down for your key fact or line. A pause before it makes it land.
- **Volume.** Speak to the back of the room.
- **Stance.** Feet planted, hands free to gesture. No swaying or pacing.
- **Time.** Aim to finish between 2:45 and 3:00. Going over the time limit costs points, and ending far under looks underprepared.

Practice with a timer. The more often you deliver the same outline, the less you need the notes.

## Strong example
The speaker's card says: "Hook: 'Since Sept 2025, AGOA lapsed once, patched twice.' 1. Short renewals, no factories (World Bank projected Lesotho apparel exports would fall 16%). 2. China: $141B exports to Africa in 8 months (CFR). Close: 'Give investors a date they can build on.'" Every point is there, and none of it can be read word for word.

## Weak example
Two full pages typed in small print. The speaker reads them with their head down, loses their place once and runs to 3:20.

## Try it
Pick one of your speech plans and cut it to an outline that fits on a single card. Then open the practice timer, deliver it three times, and note your time and how often you looked down.

# Training lesson: Presiding officer basics
- Track: delivery
- Skill: Run the floor fairly with precedence, recency and clear procedure.
- Drill: none
- Bill section: none

## Lesson
The **presiding officer (PO)** is a student who runs the session. POs are judged and ranked like everyone else, and a strong PO often ranks high. The job is to be fair, efficient and in control.

**Recognizing speakers.** Use **precedence and recency**. Precedence: people with fewer speeches go first. Recency: among equals, the person who spoke longest ago goes first. Keep a chart so you can show your choices are fair. Many tournaments also use a separate list for questioners.

**Running the floor.** A PO:

- Calls for speakers, alternating affirmative and negative.
- Times speeches and questioning, and gives time signals.
- Handles **motions**: to move the previous question (end debate), to amend, to recess. Know which ones need a second and what vote they need under your tournament's rules.
- Keeps things moving: short transitions, no long pauses.

**Staying neutral.** The PO doesn't take sides or debate. A quick, consistent, polite PO keeps the chamber running and gets noticed by judges.

## Strong example
"Thank you, Representative. That concludes the questioning period. We're now looking for a negative speaker. I see Representatives Ortiz, Chen and Patel. Representative Chen, you have the floor, with precedence." Clear, quick and fair, and the PO explains the choice.

## Weak example
"Um, OK, who wants to go next? I guess... you? Did you already speak? Never mind, go ahead." No system, so the chamber starts to doubt the PO's fairness.

## Try it
Write a one-page PO chart for a chamber of 12 legislators, with columns for speeches given and the last time each person spoke. Then practice saying the script for recognizing a speaker and for calling a vote on the previous question.
