# Training lesson: Cite evidence out loud
- Track: evidence
- Skill: Introduce a source so judges know who said it, when, and why to trust it.
- Drill: bills
- Bill section: evidence

## Lesson
In Congress, the judge can't see your sources, so you have to cite them **out loud**. A good oral citation gives three things in one breath:

- **Who:** the organization or author. "The Congressional Research Service" or "economists at Harvard."
- **When:** the year, or the month if it's recent. Old data on a fast-changing topic is a weakness, so say the date.
- **What:** the fact, number or short quote.

Add **why to trust them** when it helps: "the nonpartisan Congressional Budget Office," "the industry's own trade group" (which you might want the other side to notice).

Numbers stick when you make them human-sized. "$762 billion in trade" is hard to picture; "Canada buys more American goods than any other country" lands.

Keep the **full citation** (author, title, publication, date and link) in your prep, in case a judge or questioner asks. The app's evidence cards have a Copy citation button for exactly that.

## Strong example
"According to a 2024 study by Harvard economist Gabriel Chodorow-Reich and colleagues, published by the National Bureau of Economic Research, the 2017 tax cut raised investment about 20% at a typical affected firm."

## Weak example
"Studies show that tax cuts increase investment by a lot." No source, no date, no number, and a questioner can take it apart with "Which study?"

## Try it
Open a bill's evidence section and pick two cards. For each, write the exact sentence you would say in a speech, with who, when and what. Read them aloud; each should take under 15 seconds.

# Training lesson: Use evidence honestly
- Track: evidence
- Skill: Say only what your source supports, and check it before you rely on it.
- Drill: bills
- Bill section: evidence

## Lesson
Evidence is only as strong as its fit with your claim. Judges and good questioners catch overclaiming, and one caught stretch can cost you the credibility of the whole speech.

Before you use a card, check:

- **Does the source say exactly this?** A study that found "no clear effect" doesn't prove "no effect." A projection isn't a fact.
- **Is it current?** A 2017 cost estimate under a 35% tax rate isn't a direct estimate of a 2026 bill. Say so, or find something newer.
- **Who wrote it?** Advocacy groups, trade groups and think tanks can be good sources, but name them, so you aren't caught hiding it.
- **Does it cut both ways?** Many good studies help both sides. Know the part the other side will quote.

AI tools can invent sources, quotes and numbers that look real. Never use a card you haven't opened and checked yourself, even if it's in the app. That includes AI Learning Aids.

## Strong example
"RAND, a nonpartisan research organization, reviewed the research and called the evidence on assault weapon bans and mass shootings 'inconclusive.' That doesn't mean the ban fails; it means the affirmative can't claim it's proven to work."

## Weak example
"RAND proved the assault weapons ban doesn't work." RAND said the evidence was inconclusive, not that the ban failed. An informed questioner will ask for the exact words.

## Try it
Open a bill's evidence section, pick one card and open its link. Find the sentence in the source that supports the card. If the card says more than the source does, edit it, or add a "Watch out" note.
