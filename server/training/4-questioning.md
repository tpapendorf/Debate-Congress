# Training lesson: Ask questions in lines
- Track: questioning
- Skill: Plan a short line of questions that leads the speaker to an admission.
- Drill: bills
- Bill section: question

## Lesson
A single open question ("What do you think about the cost?") lets the speaker give a mini speech. A **line of questions** walks them, one short step at a time, to a point you can use in your own speech.

A line has three steps:

1. **Set up:** a fact they have to agree with. "Section 1 applies the 15% rate to the corporation, correct?"
2. **Lock in:** narrow it. "And it doesn't limit the rate to manufacturing income?"
3. **Pay off:** the consequence. "So a company with one factory pays 15% on all its profits?"

Rules that make lines work:

- **Yes-or-no questions.** Keep each one under ten seconds.
- **Use the bill's text.** Quote the section. Speakers can't argue with their own bill.
- **Don't ask the last question if you already have the point.** Save the conclusion for your speech.
- **Be courteous.** A calm, polite question looks stronger than a gotcha.

Questioning is scored, and it's one of the best ways to stand out on a bill you aren't speaking on.

## Strong example
"Representative, the bill enforces itself through 'the impeachment of any official found in conflict,' correct?" "Yes." "Can a law require the House to impeach someone?" "Well, no." "So what actually enforces this bill?" The speaker has to admit the enforcement section doesn't work.

## Weak example
"Don't you think it's kind of a problem that your bill might not work because of all the issues with how it's enforced and also the Constitution?" Long, vague and easy to dodge.

## Try it
Open a bill's Likely questions section. Pick one question and write a three-step line around it: set up, lock in, pay off. Make each question answerable with yes or no.

# Training lesson: Answer questions well
- Track: questioning
- Skill: Give short, direct answers that keep control without dodging.
- Drill: bills
- Bill section: question

## Lesson
When you're questioned, judges score how you answer, not only your speech. Strong answers are:

- **Short.** Answer in one or two sentences, then stop. Long answers eat the clock and look like dodging.
- **Direct.** If the answer is yes, say yes, then add the context. "Yes, and that's why..." is stronger than avoiding the word.
- **Honest.** If you don't know, say so and give what you do know. Making something up is the worst outcome.
- **On your ground.** Bring the answer back to your strongest point when you can.

Prepare for the questions you'll get. For every speech, write down the three hardest questions the other side could ask and a one-sentence answer to each.

If a questioner interrupts or talks over you, stay calm. "If I could finish my answer, Representative..." usually wins the room.

## Strong example
Question: "Does your bill ban large-capacity magazines?" Answer: "Section 1 reinstates the provisions of the 1994 Act, and the policy is the full 1994 law, including its magazine limits. If the wording needs to be clearer, I'd welcome a friendly amendment."

## Weak example
Question: "Does your bill ban large-capacity magazines?" Answer: "Well, the important thing to remember is that gun violence is a very serious issue in this country, and we have to do something about it, because..." It doesn't answer the question, and the judge notices.

## Try it
Pick a speech you plan to give. Write the three hardest questions the other side could ask you and a one- or two-sentence answer to each. Practice saying them aloud.
