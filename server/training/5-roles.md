# Training lesson: The authorship or sponsorship speech
- Track: roles
- Skill: Open debate by explaining the problem, what the bill does and why it works.
- Drill: prep
- Bill section: aff

## Lesson
The first speech on a bill sets up the whole debate. Judges expect it to do three jobs:

- **The problem.** What's wrong today, with a fact or two that shows it's real.
- **The bill.** What it does, in plain words, section by section if it helps. You should know the text better than anyone.
- **Why it works.** One or two arguments for how the bill fixes the problem.

Because the first speaker can't refute anyone yet, a strong authorship *anticipates* the other side: "Some will say this costs too much. Here's why it's worth it."

The authorship or sponsorship usually gets the longest questioning period. Expect questions about the bill's text and its weak spots, and prepare answers for each. If the bill has a drafting problem, have a calm, honest answer ready, such as offering a friendly amendment.

## Strong example
"Since September 2025, AGOA has lapsed once and been patched twice. Every time it lapses, factories in places like Lesotho lose orders. This bill does three things: it extends AGOA to 2041, funds a $250 million a year program to help countries that barely use it, and gives countries 180 days to fix problems before losing access. I'll explain why each matters..."

## Weak example
"This bill is about AGOA, which is a trade program. It's very important. I think we should pass it because trade is good and Africa is important." It doesn't explain the problem, the bill or how it works, and questioners will ask what the bill actually does.

## Try it
Pick a current bill and plan an authorship or sponsorship. In My prep, start an Authorship speech plan. Write one line for the problem (with a fact), three lines on what the bill does, and your two arguments. List the two hardest questions you expect.

# Training lesson: Mid-round and crystallization speeches
- Track: roles
- Skill: Give later speeches that move the debate forward or sum it up.
- Drill: prep
- Bill section: none

## Lesson
Speeches later in the debate have different jobs than the first ones. Judges reward speakers who know which job their speech is doing.

**Mid-round speeches** (roughly speeches three to six) should add something the debate doesn't have yet:

- A **new argument** nobody has raised.
- **Refutation** of the other side's strongest point, by name.
- **New evidence** on a point that's being argued without proof.

**Late speeches and crystallization** (when debate is close to ending) should *pull the debate together*:

- Name the **two or three main clashes**: the questions the two sides actually disagree on.
- For each one, say **who's winning and why**, naming speakers on both sides.
- **Weigh**: explain why your side's impacts matter more (bigger, more likely, sooner, or affecting more people).
- End with a clear reason to vote your way.

A crystallization that only repeats what your side said isn't crystallizing. It has to deal fairly with the other side's best points too.

## Strong example
"Representatives, this debate comes down to two questions. First, does the bill's enforcement work? Representative Park says courts will enforce it, but as Representative Diaz showed, the bill never mentions courts. Second..." The speaker names the clashes and the people on both sides.

## Weak example
"Let me remind you of all the great affirmative arguments: jobs, the Constitution, trade, our allies..." A list of labels, with no clash and no weighing.

## Try it
Pick a bill you know well. Write the two main clashes as questions, one line each. For each, write one sentence on why your side wins it. In My prep, save it as a plan for a late speech.
