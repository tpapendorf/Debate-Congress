# Training lesson: Claim, warrant, impact
- Track: arguments
- Skill: Build every point from a claim, a reason it's true and a reason it matters.
- Drill: bills
- Bill section: aff

## Lesson
Every point in a speech needs three parts:

- **Claim:** what you're arguing, in one sentence.
- **Warrant:** *why* it's true. This is your reasoning or evidence. Without it, the claim is just an opinion.
- **Impact:** why it matters, and to whom. This is what judges weigh when two sides disagree.

The most common mistake is skipping the warrant. "This bill helps the economy" is a claim. "Because companies decide where to build by comparing after-tax profits, a lower rate makes U.S. plants more attractive" is the warrant that makes judges believe it.

A good test: after each point, ask "says who?" and "so what?" If you can't answer "says who," you need a warrant. If you can't answer "so what," you need an impact.

Make impacts concrete. "Jobs" is vague. "Apparel jobs in Lesotho, most of them held by women, that disappear when duty-free access lapses" is something a judge can picture.

## Strong example
**Claim:** Short AGOA renewals discourage the factories the program is meant to attract.
**Warrant:** An investor building a plant needs duty-free access to last longer than the time it takes to earn back the cost, and two-year extensions don't.
**Impact:** The jobs never get created, in some of the poorest countries in the world.

## Weak example
"AGOA is good for Africa and good for America, so we should extend it. It helps trade and it helps people." Two claims, no warrant, and the impact is too vague to weigh.

## Try it
Open a bill and read its affirmative arguments. Pick one and rewrite it in three labeled lines: Claim, Warrant, Impact. Then do the same for one negative argument.

# Training lesson: Refutation: answer the debate, don't restart it
- Track: arguments
- Skill: Answer a specific speaker's point in four steps, so your speech clashes.
- Drill: bills
- Bill section: neg

## Lesson
After the first two or three speeches on a bill, judges expect **clash**: you engage with what's already been said. A speech that ignores earlier speakers sounds pre-written, even if it's good.

Use four steps to refute a point:

1. **They say:** name the speaker and their point in one sentence. "Representative Kim told us the tariff limits protect consumers."
2. **But:** your answer, in one sentence.
3. **Because:** your warrant or evidence.
4. **Therefore:** why your answer wins, and what it means for the vote.

You don't need to refute everything. Pick the point the other side relies on most, and take it apart. Then add something new so your speech moves the debate forward.

Keep a **flow** during the session: a sheet with a column for each speech, where you jot each speaker's main points. That's where your refutation comes from.

## Strong example
"Representative Kim told us the bill protects consumers from tariffs. But the bill only requires a vote of Congress; it doesn't ban a single tariff. Because Congress could still pass the same tariffs, consumers are protected only if Congress votes differently than the President would. So the real question is who decides, and that's where I'll focus."

## Weak example
"The other side is wrong about consumers. Tariffs are actually good. Now my first point..." No name, no reason, and it moves on before the answer lands.

## Try it
Open a bill and pick one argument from the other side. Write a refutation in four lines: They say, But, Because, Therefore. Read it aloud and time it; aim for under 30 seconds.
